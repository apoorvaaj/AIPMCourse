In Module 1 you decided whether a problem needs artificial intelligence (AI). This module is about how. Engineers will offer you choices with unfamiliar names: prompting, retrieval, fine-tuning, a bigger model, an agent. Each choice changes what the product costs, how fast it answers, how easy it is to keep up to date, and how it fails. Those are product decisions, so you need to understand them well enough to ask the right questions and defend an answer. You will not write any code.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  1. Choose between prompting, retrieval, fine-tuning, classical machine learning and agents for a given problem, and defend the choice.
  2. Trade off quality, cost and response time to pick a model, and calculate what a design costs to run.
  3. Read a system diagram and spot where it will fail, and judge which kind of prediction error matters more for a given action.

Step 1 · Lesson · about 90 minutesHow AI systems are built, and what that means for you

Parts 1 to 3 explain how a large language model works and the five main ways to build a feature with one. Part 4 covers choosing a model and working out its cost. Part 5 covers prediction models and their two kinds of error. Part 6 shows you how to record a technical decision so others can check it.

2.1 How a large language model reads and writes

A large language model (LLM) is a generative AI model trained on huge amounts of text. Claude and GPT are examples. When you send it a request, it reads everything you gave it, then writes an answer one small piece at a time, each time choosing a likely next piece. Five ideas follow from this, and each one has a product consequence.

Token
The small piece of text a model reads and writes. In English, one token is roughly three-quarters of a word, so 1,000 tokens is about 750 words. Many other languages and scripts, including Tamil, often take more tokens for the same meaning. Why you care: you pay per token, and limits are set in tokens.
Input and output tokens
Input is everything the model reads: your instructions, any documents, the conversation so far and the user's question. Output is what it writes. Output tokens usually cost several times more than input tokens.
Context window
The most tokens a model can consider in one request, counting input and output together. It works like a desk: everything the model needs must fit on it at once. Why you care: a bigger desk costs more to fill, and models can overlook details buried in the middle of a very long input.
Training cutoff
The date after which the model learned nothing new. It does not know about a leaflet updated last week unless you give it that leaflet in the request.
Hallucination
A fluent, confident answer that is not supported by any source. It happens because the model is choosing likely words, not looking up facts. Why you care: this is the failure you will design against most often.
Response time (latency)
How long the user waits for an answer. It grows with the size of the model and with the number of tokens it must read and write.

The big idea. A model only knows two things: what it learned in training, and what you put on its desk for this request. Most design choices in this module are about what goes on the desk.

2.2 Five ways to build an AI feature

Think of the options as a ladder. Each rung adds capability, but also cost, complexity and new ways to fail. Start on the lowest rung that could work, and climb only when testing shows you must. This is the core advice in Anthropic's widely read guide to building with LLMs, listed in the reading.

  1. 1Prompting onlyGive a general model clear instructions and the user's input. It answers from what it learned in training.
  2. 2Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)Before the model answers, a search step finds the most relevant passages from your own approved documents and puts them on the desk. The model answers from those and can show which document it used.
  3. 3Fine-tuningTrain an existing model further on hundreds or thousands of your own examples, so its default behaviour changes.
  4. 4Tool use and fixed workflowsThe model can ask your software to do things (look up a record, check a calendar) inside steps your developers have fixed in advance.
  5. 5AgentsA model using tools in a loop and deciding its own next step until the task is done. Most flexible, least predictable, hardest to test.

Classical machine learning (ML) sits beside the ladder rather than on it. When the job is to predict a number or a category from structured records, such as "will this patient miss their appointment?", a classical model is usually cheaper, faster and easier to test than an LLM. Part 2.5 covers it.

Two building blocks appear inside several of these approaches:

Structured output
Asking the model to answer in a fixed format with named fields, often JSON (JavaScript Object Notation, a standard text format for data). Software can then check each field, for example that a dose field contains a number found in the leaflet.
Guardrail
A check before or after the model, often a plain rule rather than AI. For example: if the question mentions chest pain, skip the model and tell the user to call for help.

Worked example · Riverside Public Library (fictional)

The library wants an "Ask the library" chat in its app. Members ask about opening hours, borrowing limits, fines and how to book rooms. These rules are written in a 40-page policy handbook that changes about four times a year.

  • Prompting only would answer from general knowledge of how libraries usually work. It might say members can borrow 10 books when Riverside allows 8. Rejected.
  • Fine-tuning on the handbook would need retraining every time the handbook changes, and could not show members the rule it used. Rejected.
  • Retrieval over the handbook answers from the current rules and can quote the section. A guardrail sends any question about a disputed fine to a person. Chosen.
  • An agent that could also renew loans and waive fines was considered and deferred: waiving fines is an action with a cost, and the library wants six months of chat logs first.

Notice the pattern: the choice was driven by how often the facts change, whether users need to see the source, and what an action would cost if it went wrong.

2.3 How retrieval works, and how to read a system diagram

Retrieval depends on embeddings. An embedding turns a piece of text into a long list of numbers that captures its meaning, so that texts with similar meaning get similar numbers. That lets the search step find "what to do after a missed dose" when a patient types "I forgot my pill this morning", even though the two share no words. This is often called search by meaning, or semantic search. The documents are cut into short passages (called chunks), each passage gets an embedding, and the embeddings are kept in a searchable store called a vector database.

The failure-point walk

To spot where a design will fail, walk through each box and ask four questions. You do not need to know how the box is built.

  1. What goes in? Could it be missing, wrong, out of date or in an unexpected language?
  2. What comes out? What does a bad output look like?
  3. How could this box get it wrong? Name the most likely and the most harmful error.
  4. How would we know? Is there a check after it, a log, a person? If nobody would notice, that is your weakest point.

For a retrieval design, the same five failure points come up again and again:

2.4 Choosing a model: quality, cost and response time

Vendors usually offer a family of models in several sizes. Larger models tend to handle harder reasoning better, but they cost more per token and take longer to answer. You are always trading three things against each other: quality, cost and response time.

  • Pick the smallest model that passes your test set. "Best on a public benchmark" is not the same as "good enough for our task". Test on your own questions.
  • Route. Send easy questions to a small model and hard ones to a bigger one, or to a person.
  • Watch where the data goes. Some models are only available in certain countries or data centres. A model that sends patient data overseas may be ruled out before quality is even considered.
  • Plan for price changes. Model prices have moved a lot, in both directions. A design should still make sense if prices rise by half.

Working out what a design costs to run

Prices are quoted per million tokens, with separate prices for input and output. The cost of one request is:

cost per request = (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price) ÷ 1,000,000

Then multiply by how many requests you expect. Add fixed monthly costs that do not depend on use, such as a vector database or dedicated hosting.

Worked example · the library chat

Each question puts about 1,500 input tokens on the desk (instructions 600, three handbook passages 850, the question 50) and gets an answer of about 200 output tokens. The library's chosen model costs S$3 (Singapore dollars) per million input tokens and S$15 per million output tokens.

(1,500 × 3 + 200 × 15) ÷ 1,000,000 = (4,500 + 3,000) ÷ 1,000,000 = S$0.0075 per question

That is S$7.50 per 1,000 questions. At 5,000 questions a month, the model costs about S$37.50 a month. Add S$100 a month for the vector database and the total is about S$137.50.

The lesson inside the arithmetic: token costs are often small next to the cost of the people who build, review and support the product. But they grow in direct proportion to use, and design choices multiply them. Doubling the passages retrieved, or moving to a model five times the price, changes the number fast.

2.5 Prediction models and their two kinds of error

A classical prediction model gives each case a score, for example the chance that a reserved book will never be collected. You choose a threshold: cases scoring above it are flagged. Every flag then lands in one of four boxes.

Really happened
Did not happen
Flagged
120Correct flag (true positive)
30False alarm (false positive)
Not flagged
80Missed case (false negative)
770Correctly left alone (true negative)

The numbers are from the library: 1,000 reservations, of which 200 were never collected. The model flagged 150.

Precision
Of everything flagged, how much was right? 120 ÷ 150 = 80%. Low precision means many false alarms.
Recall
Of everything that really happened, how much did we catch? 120 ÷ 200 = 60%. Low recall means many missed cases.
False alarm rate
Of the cases that were fine, how many did we wrongly flag? 30 ÷ 800 = 3.75%. This matters when false alarms land on people.
Base rate
How common the event is: here 200 in 1,000, or 20%.

Three rules for product managers

  1. Never accept "accuracy" on its own. If only 11% of appointments are missed, a "model" that predicts "everyone attends" is 89% accurate and completely useless. Ask for precision and recall.
  2. The threshold is a product decision, not a technical one. Lowering it catches more real cases (higher recall) but raises more false alarms (lower precision). Raising it does the opposite. You cannot have both at their best.
  3. Which error matters more depends on the action you take. If a flag triggers something cheap and harmless, such as a reminder message, false alarms cost little, so favour recall. If a flag triggers something costly or unfair to the person flagged, favour precision, or reconsider the action.

Finally, always compare the model with a baseline: the simplest rule that does the same job, such as "flag anyone who did this before". A model has to beat the baseline by enough to pay for itself.

2.6 Recording the decision: the architecture decision record

An architecture decision record (ADR) is a short document that captures one important design choice: the situation, the options considered, what was chosen, and what the team accepts as a result. Engineering teams have used them for years. They matter even more for AI products, because the right choice depends on prices, models and test results that will change. A good ADR lets someone, a year later, see why you chose what you did and know when to choose again.

SectionWhat it says
ContextThe problem and the constraints, in a few sentences
Decision driversThe three to five things that matter most, in priority order (for example: patient safety, ease of updating, cost)
Options consideredEach option with its evidence: cost, test results, main failure. A table works well.
DecisionWhat you chose, in one or two sentences
ConsequencesWhat you gain, what you give up, the risks you accept and how you will control them
Revisit whenSpecific events that would reopen the decision, for example "a model price rises by more than 50%"

Worked example · ADR-007, Riverside "Ask the library" chat (shortened)

Context. Members ask staff about 5,000 policy questions a month. Rules live in a handbook revised four times a year.

Drivers. 1. Answers match current policy. 2. Members can see the rule. 3. Under S$500 a month to run.

Options. Prompting only: about S$25 a month in tokens, but 31% of test answers contradicted the handbook. Fine-tuning: cannot show sources, retraining four times a year at S$2,000 each. Retrieval: S$137.50 a month, 94% of test answers correct with the right section named.

Decision. Retrieval over the handbook, with fine disputes sent to staff.

Consequences. We accept the cost of a vector database and must keep the handbook current. The handbook owner signs off each revision before it is re-indexed.

Revisit when. Correct answers drop below 90% in the weekly review; or we want the chat to take actions such as renewing loans.

Leaflet 1 · Calvexin 5 mg tablets · last reviewed June 2026
FICTIONAL MEDICINE FOR TEACHING. NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.
Calvexin lowers blood pressure. Take one tablet each morning, with or without food. If you miss a dose, take it as soon as you remember on the same day. If you only remember the next day, skip the missed dose. Never take two doses together. Common side effects: ankle swelling, flushing, headache. Avoid drinking large amounts of grapefruit juice.

Leaflet 2 · Glimora 500 mg tablets · last reviewed April 2026
FICTIONAL MEDICINE FOR TEACHING. NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.
Glimora helps control blood sugar. Take one tablet twice a day with breakfast and dinner. If you miss a dose, take it with your next meal only if that meal is within 4 hours of the usual time; otherwise skip it. Common side effect: stomach upset. If you feel shaky, sweaty or confused, eat something sugary and tell your doctor.

Leaflet 3 · Restolin preventer inhaler · last reviewed May 2026
FICTIONAL MEDICINE FOR TEACHING. NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.
Restolin prevents asthma symptoms over time. Use two puffs every morning and evening, even when you feel well. Rinse your mouth with water after each use. Restolin does not relieve a sudden asthma attack. In an attack, use your reliever inhaler. If breathing does not improve, call 995.

Leaflet 4 · Pravadex 20 mg tablets · last reviewed January 2026
FICTIONAL MEDICINE FOR TEACHING. NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.
Pravadex lowers cholesterol. Take one tablet each night. If you miss a dose, skip it and take the next one at the usual time. Tell your doctor straight away about any unexplained muscle pain or weakness.

Leaflet 5 · Duroxal 500 mg tablets · last reviewed March 2023 REVIEW OVERDUE
FICTIONAL MEDICINE FOR TEACHING. NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.
Duroxal relieves pain and fever. Take one or two tablets up to four times a day, at least 4 hours apart. Do not take more than 8 tablets in 24 hours. Many cold and flu products contain the same ingredient; check before taking them together.

Going furtherReading and practice ideas

Reading

  • Anthropic, Building effective agents (2024). Explains the difference between fixed workflows and agents, and why to start with the simplest design. About 30 minutes. Skip the code if you like.
  • Eugene Yan, Patterns for Building LLM-based Systems & Products. Seven building blocks, including retrieval, fine-tuning, guardrails and caching. Long; read the introduction and the sections on retrieval and fine-tuning. About 45 minutes.

Practise on your own world

Try the ladder and the error questions on one of these ideas, or on a product from your own workplace, and bring it to your viva if you like.

  • Staff policy assistant: answers employees' questions about leave, travel and expense rules. Prompting, retrieval or fine-tuning? What happens when a policy changes?
  • Payment fraud flag: flags unusual payments for review before release. Which error costs more: a missed fraud or a delayed genuine payment? Who bears each?
  • Supplier email sorter: reads incoming supplier emails and routes them to the right team. Would a classical model, an LLM or simple rules do best?
