Most software does exactly what it was told. Software built on artificial intelligence (AI) does what it has learned, which is usually right and sometimes wrong in ways nobody wrote down. This module shows you what that means for the person deciding what to build, and gives you a simple, repeatable test for deciding whether a problem needs AI at all.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  1. Explain how AI products differ from rule-based software: their outputs vary, they depend on data, and they need testing for as long as they run.
  2. Map the AI product lifecycle from first idea to live monitoring, and name the product decision made at each stage.
  3. Decide whether a problem needs AI at all, and name the cheaper alternative when it does not.

Step 1 · Lesson · 75–90 minutesWhat changes when the product is AI

The lesson has six parts. Parts 1 to 3 explain what is different about AI products and the people who manage them. Parts 4 to 6 give you the tools you will use in the assignment: the lifecycle, the cost-of-error grid and the AI-or-not test.

1.1 Two versions of the same feature

Imagine a clinic that sends appointment reminders. Here are two ways to build the feature.

Version 1 · Rule-based

A programmer writes a rule: three days before the appointment, send this message.

Reminder: you have an appointment at Bedok Clinic on Thu 6 Nov, 9:30am. Reply C to cancel.

Every patient gets the same wording. If it works for one patient it works for all of them. You test it once.

Version 2 · AI-written

An AI model writes a friendly, personal reminder for each patient from their booking details.

Hi Mr Tan! See you at Bedok Clinic on Thursday morning for your diabetes review. Please remember to fast for 8 hours before.

Most messages are better than Version 1. But this one invented a fasting instruction nobody asked for. Testing one message tells you very little.

Version 2 is not broken. It is doing what AI does: producing the most likely good answer, which is usually right and occasionally confidently wrong. That single difference is the root of almost everything in this programme.

A few terms you will meet throughout the course:

Artificial intelligence (AI)
Software that performs tasks we associate with human judgement, such as understanding language or spotting patterns, by learning from examples rather than following only hand-written rules.
Model
The learned part of an AI system. It takes an input (a question, a record) and produces an output (an answer, a prediction).
Training data
The examples a model learned from. What is in the data, and what is missing, shapes how the model behaves.
Machine learning (ML)
The family of methods that lets software learn patterns from data. Most AI today is built with machine learning.
Classical machine learning
Models that predict a number or a category from structured data, such as "will this patient miss their appointment: yes or no". Usually cheaper and easier to test.
Generative AI
Models that create new content: text, images, audio. Their outputs are open-ended, which makes them more flexible and harder to check.
Large language model (LLM)
A generative AI model trained on very large amounts of text, such as Claude or GPT. It can answer questions, summarise and draft. From here on we write LLM.
Prompt
The instructions and context given to an LLM. Changing a single word in a prompt can change the output.

1.2 Four ways AI products differ from rule-based software

DifferenceRule-based softwareAI productWhat it means for you
1. Outputs varySame input, same output, every timeSimilar inputs can get different outputs; quality is spread across a rangeYou specify how often it must be right and what it must never do, not just what it does
2. Behaviour comes from dataBehaviour is set by code people wrote and can readBehaviour is shaped by training data, prompts and the documents the model readsData quality, coverage and permission to use it become product decisions
3. Quality is never finishedOnce tested, it keeps working until someone changes itQuality shifts when the model is updated, users change, or the world changes (called drift)Testing continues after launch; monitoring is part of the product
4. Costs grow with useRunning cost barely changes per extra userEvery request costs money to processPopularity can make a product unaffordable; you model cost per task early

The big idea. In rule-based software, a wrong answer is a bug you find and fix once. In an AI product, a wrong answer is a rate you measure and manage for the life of the product.

1.3 What an AI product manager does

A product manager is responsible for deciding what gets built and why. In any product, they manage four classic risks: will people value it, can they use it, can we build it, and does it work for the business. An AI product manager does all of that, plus five jobs that ordinary software rarely demands:

  1. Decide whether AI fits at all. Many problems are solved better by a rule, a form or a process change.
  2. Define "good enough". Set the quality bar in numbers, such as "correct in at least 98 of 100 test cases, and never gives a dose that differs from the label".
  3. Decide how much the AI may do alone. Suggest only, act with approval, or act alone. This depends on what an error costs.
  4. Manage the cost to run. Know what one task costs and what happens to the budget if usage triples.
  5. Own new kinds of risk. Safety, fairness between groups of users, privacy, and regulation.

You will rarely do this alone. The table shows who you typically work with.

1.4 The AI product lifecycle

A lifecycle is the series of stages a product moves through, from first idea to retirement. The AI product lifecycle has seven stages. What makes it different is not the stages themselves but how often you go back.

  1. Stage 1OpportunityIs there a problem worth solving?
  2. Stage 2Fit and feasibilityDoes AI suit it, and is the data usable?
  3. Stage 3PrototypeDoes it work on real examples?
  4. Stage 4Specify and evaluateWhat is good enough, and how do we test it?
  5. Stage 5Build and integrateDoes it work inside the real workflow?
  6. Stage 6Launch in stagesIs it safe and useful for real users?
  7. Stage 7Monitor and improveIs it still good enough?

↺ Any stage can send you back. A poor prototype sends you back to Stage 2. A drop in quality after launch sends you back to Stage 4. Stage 7 never ends while the product is live.

StageThe decision you makeWhat you produceSignal to stop or go backTaught in
1. OpportunityPursue the problem or drop itProblem statement with evidenceNo clear, frequent user painModule 3
2. Fit and feasibilityUse AI, use a non-AI solution, or combine themAI-or-not assessment, data checkA cheaper option works; the data is missing or not allowedModules 1, 2, 3
3. PrototypeContinue, change approach or stopPrototype and reviewed sample outputsOutputs are poor even on easy casesModule 8
4. Specify and evaluateSet the quality bar and the "must never" rulesRequirements and a test setThe bar cannot be reached at an acceptable costModules 4, 5, 6
5. Build and integrateApprove the release planWorking system with safety checksIt does not fit how staff or patients actually workModule 7

7. Monitor and improveAdjust, retrain, replace or retireLive quality and cost reports, incident logDrift, cost overrun, a model update that changes behaviourModules 6, 9

Notice that Stage 2 is where this module lives. A good decision here is the cheapest decision you will ever make: stopping a weak idea at Stage 2 costs a few days, while stopping it at Stage 6 can cost a year and a damaged reputation.

1.5 The cost of a wrong answer

Every AI feature will sometimes be wrong. So the useful question is not "will it make mistakes?" but three sharper ones:

  1. How bad is a wrong answer? An odd book suggestion costs nothing. A wrong medicine dose can harm someone.
  2. Who bears the cost? Often it is not the person who benefits. A tool might save staff time while patients carry the risk.
  3. Can someone catch it in time? A draft a doctor reads before signing is different from an instant reply a patient acts on alone.

Answers to the first and third questions place a feature on this grid, and the grid suggests a design.

Easy to catch in time
Hard to catch in time
Low cost if wrong
AutomateLet the AI act. Fix errors as they come up.
Automate and watchLet the AI act, but check a sample of outputs regularly.
High cost if wrong
AI suggests, a person decidesA qualified person approves every output.
Avoid, or narrow the scopeDo not use AI, or cut the task down until errors become catchable.

A trap to watch for. "A person reviews it" only works if the person has the time and the habit to check properly. When the AI is usually right, reviewers start to trust it and skim. This is called automation bias, and it is why human review is a design you test, not a box you tick.

1.6 The AI-or-not test

Here is the tool you will use in the assignment. Score an idea from 1 to 5 on five questions. A higher score always favours using AI.

CriterionThe questionScore 1 meansScore 5 means
C1. Problem valueHow painful, frequent and measurable is the problem?Minor or rareSevere, frequent, with clear numbers
C2. Advantage over non-AIHow much better is AI than the best simple alternative (a rule, form, search or process change)?A simple option works just as wellNo simple option comes close
C3. Error toleranceUsing the grid above: how costly and catchable are wrong answers?Serious harm, hard to catchCheap, or reliably caught in time
C4. Data readinessIs there enough good data that we are allowed to use?Missing, poor or not permittedPlentiful, good and permitted
C5. Value versus costDoes the value clearly exceed the cost to build, run and govern it?Cost exceeds valueClear margin, even if costs rise

Turning scores into a verdict

Total (out of 25)VerdictWhat it means
21–25GoProceed to prototype
16–20Go with conditionsProceed with a narrow scope, a pilot or a named safeguard
11–15Not yetFix the weakest criterion first, then score again
5–10No-goDrop it, or solve it another way

Two red-flag rules override the total.

  • Any criterion scored 1 is a red flag. The verdict cannot be better than Not yet, however high the total.
  • If the red flag is on C2, the verdict is No-go: use the non-AI alternative. The problem may be real, but AI is not the answer.

Scores are judgements, not facts. That is fine, as long as every score has one line of evidence and you say which ones are assumptions.

Worked example · Riverside Public Library (a different, fictional organisation)

A library considers two ideas: AI book recommendations in its app, and AI to calculate overdue fines.

IdeaC1C2C3C4C5TotalVerdict
Book recommendations3354318Go with conditions
Overdue fines2125111No-go: use a rule

Recommendations. Members often ask staff for "something like this" (C1: 3). Staff-picked lists exist but are not personal (C2: 3). A poor suggestion costs nothing (C3: 5). There are five years of borrowing records, but they must be anonymised first (C4: 4). An off-the-shelf tool keeps cost moderate (C5: 3). Condition: pilot with anonymised data in one branch.

Fines. A fine is days late multiplied by a daily rate. A one-line rule does this perfectly (C2: 1, red flag), and a wrong AI-calculated fine would cause disputes (C3: 2). The data is perfect (C4: 5), which shows that good data alone never justifies AI.

Four common mistakes

  • Scoring the technology instead of the case. Ask what this AI would do for these users with this data, not what AI can do in general.
  • Forgetting who bears the error. If staff benefit and patients carry the risk, say so.
  • Treating it as all or nothing. Often the best answer is a mix: rules for the risky part, AI for the rest.
  • Assuming data is ready because it exists. Check quality, coverage, language and whether you are allowed to use it.

The five fact sheets

Open each sheet. As you read, note for each one: the user problem, the non-AI option, the cost of a wrong answer, and who bears it.

A. Medicine questions assistantLLM answering patients about their own medicines
Problem today
The pharmacy helpline receives about 1,900 calls a week. Peak waits reach 25 minutes. Around 60% of calls are one of five questions: what a medicine is for, when to take it, what to do after a missed dose, side effects, and whether it is safe with another medicine.
Proposed AI
A chat assistant in the app that answers using the patient's prescription record and the group's approved medicine leaflets.
Non-AI option
A searchable leaflet library in the app in all four languages, plus a "message a pharmacist" form with a four-hour reply target.
Data
640 approved leaflets. All exist in English; about 70% are translated. About 8% are overdue for their scheduled review. Prescription records are already in the app.
If it is wrong
Wrong advice on a dose, a missed dose or a drug combination could harm a patient, who may act on it without asking anyone.
Estimated cost
S$350,000 to build; S$60,000 a year to run.
Estimated value
If 40% of routine calls move to the app, about 2.5 full-time pharmacist roles are freed for clinical work.
"Half my team's day is answering the same five questions. But the sixth question, the one that isn't routine, is the one that matters."Head Pharmacist
B. Booking and symptom-routing chatbotLLM with rules and nurse handoff
Problem today
About 9% of bookings are for the wrong service, for example a doctor's slot booked for a vaccination. That is roughly 83,000 bookings a year, each taking about 10 minutes of staff time to fix. Separately, some patients book a routine slot for symptoms that need urgent care.
Proposed AI
A chatbot that asks about the patient's need, books the right service, and flags urgent symptoms to a nurse.
Non-AI option
A redesigned booking form with a plain-language service picker, plus a fixed set of warning-sign questions at the start (for example chest pain or breathing difficulty) that tell the patient to call 995, Singapore's emergency ambulance number.
Data
Three years of booking records. Phone calls are not recorded. The nurse triage protocol, the checklist nurses use to judge urgency, exists on paper only.
If it is wrong
A wrong service means a rebooking: low cost. A missed emergency could cause serious harm or death, borne by the patient.
Estimated cost
S$420,000 to build; S$90,000 a year to run.
Estimated value
Removing most wrong-service bookings saves about 14,000 staff hours a year.
"Booking is a puzzle. Triage is a clinical judgement. I'm nervous when people talk about them as one product."Nurse Manager, Emergency and Triage
C. Consultation note drafterSpeech-to-text plus LLM
Problem today
Doctors spend about 4.5 minutes writing notes per consultation. At around 30 consultations a day, that is over two hours. In the last staff survey, note-writing was the most-cited cause of burnout.
Proposed AI
A tool that records the consultation (with consent), turns speech into text, and drafts the visit note for the doctor to check and sign.
Non-AI option
Structured note templates and quick-text phrases. Or human medical scribes at about S$3,200 a month each.
Data
No recordings exist today. Consent would be needed every time. Many consultations mix languages, including English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil and Hokkien. A vendor product exists, so Harbourview would not train its own model.
If it is wrong
A wrong medicine, dose or allergy in the record could affect future care. The doctor reviews every draft, but under time pressure may skim.
Estimated cost
S$200,000 to set up; about S$125,000 a year in vendor fees for all 260 doctors.
Estimated value
The vendor claims a 40% cut in note time. If true, about 50 minutes a day per doctor.
"I'd love the time back. But I sign that note. If it's wrong, it's my name on it."Family Physician, Tampines Clinic
D. Missed-appointment predictorClassical machine learning
Problem today
11% of booked appointments are missed, about 101,000 a year. Each empty slot costs about S$45 in idle clinic time. All patients get one text message three days before.
Proposed AI
A model that predicts who is likely to miss an appointment, so staff can call them or book an extra patient into that slot (overbooking).
Non-AI option
A second reminder text message for everyone the day before, plus a phone call for anyone who missed their last appointment (a simple rule).
Data
Five years of appointment records with attendance, age, postcode, chronic-condition flags and past missed appointments. Postcode can act as a stand-in for income.
If it is wrong
A false alarm means an unneeded call or an overbooked slot where patients wait longer. If the model learns that poorer or older patients miss more often, those groups may be overbooked more and served worse.
Estimated cost
S$120,000 to build; S$15,000 a year to run.
Estimated value
Cutting missed appointments by a fifth recovers about S$900,000 a year in clinic time.
"We already know who misses: people juggling shifts, carers, the elderly who live alone. I'd rather help them come than punish them for not coming."Director of Operations
E. Patient feedback analyserLLM classification
Problem today
About 24,000 free-text comments arrive a year from the post-visit survey. One quality officer reads a 10% sample each month. In 2025, a complaint that a patient was given the wrong medicine went unread for six weeks.
Proposed AI
A tool that sorts every comment into themes for clinic managers and flags possible safety issues for urgent review.
Non-AI option
Add a yes/no question to the survey, "Did anything about your care feel unsafe?", which alerts the quality team, plus alerts on keywords such as "wrong medicine".
Data
Three years of comments, about 72,000. About 15% are not in English. Many contain names and phone numbers.
If it is wrong
A comment in the wrong theme matters little. A missed safety complaint matters a great deal. All outputs go to staff, not patients.
Estimated cost
S$90,000 to build; S$20,000 a year to run.
Estimated value
Every comment read within a day; managers get monthly themes for every clinic instead of a sample.
"I don't need a machine to tell me patients dislike waiting. I need it to make sure I never miss the one comment that says someone was hurt."Quality and Patient Safety Officer

Step 3 · Assignment · 3–4 hours · Graded 60%The AI-or-not audit

Your task. Write a memo of no more than two pages (about 1,000 words plus the table) recommending to the Harbourview board which initiatives to start in the next six months, which to defer, and which to drop or solve without AI.

What your memo must contain

  1. Recommendation first. In three to five sentences: which (at most two) to start, which to defer, which to drop or solve another way.
  2. Scored decision table for all five initiatives, using criteria C1 to C5 from Lesson 1.6, with totals, red flags and your verdict.
  3. One short paragraph per initiative covering: the user problem; the non-AI alternative; what AI adds beyond it; the cost of a wrong answer and who bears it; your verdict and any conditions.
  4. Assumptions and what would change your mind. Name your two or three biggest assumptions and the evidence that would reverse your recommendation.
  5. Next steps. What happens in the next 30 days for each initiative you start.

Viva · 15%

A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice") is a short spoken examination. Yours lasts 10 minutes, with an assessor or a trained peer.

MinutesWhat happens
0–2You summarise your recommendation in your own words, without reading the memo.
2–7Questions on your scores and verdicts. Expect "why a 3 and not a 2?" and "who bears that error?"
7–10A new fact you have not seen, which may change the case. You say whether it changes your recommendation and why.

It is marked on: whether you can defend your choices with evidence, whether you know your own assumptions, how you handle the new fact, and how clearly you speak. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well. Defending a weak position stubbornly does not.
