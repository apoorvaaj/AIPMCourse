1. Orientation briefing

Artificial intelligence (AI) products behave differently from the software you have managed before. Three ideas explain most of that difference, and every module returns to them.

Idea 1. Behaviour is a distribution, not a function

Rule-based software gives the same output for the same input. A product built on a large language model (LLM) gives a range of outputs, most good and some not. Changing the model version, the wording of an instruction (the prompt) or the documents it reads shifts that range.

So a product manager (PM) cannot only say what the product does. You also say what it should usually do, what it must never do, and how you will measure how often each happens. A requirement such as "summarises the draft accurately" is incomplete until it states how accuracy is checked and what failure rate is acceptable.

Idea 2. The cost of a wrong answer sets the design

Every AI feature will sometimes be wrong. The question is what a wrong answer costs, who pays it, and whether a person can catch it first. A wrong recipe suggestion costs a mildly disappointing dinner. A wrong answer about a medicine dose could harm someone.

That cost decides how much freedom the system gets. Your Attic agent proposes changes and waits for approval because deleting the wrong email is costly. Quaestio only asks questions because rewriting a student's work would breach academic integrity. Low-cost errors allow automation; high-cost errors call for human review, narrow scope, or no AI at all.

Idea 3. Evaluation is the specification

An evaluation (often shortened to "eval") is a repeatable test of whether outputs are good enough. In AI products, a well-built set of evals is the most precise statement of what the product should do: it turns "must never rewrite student text" into a check that runs on every change.

Good practice starts by reading real outputs and sorting the failures you find, before writing automated tests. Generic scores such as "helpfulness out of 10" rarely tell you what to fix.

How to use the quiz. Answer before reading the explanation. The quiz is formative: it does not count toward your grade, and getting questions wrong now is useful because it shows where Module 1 should spend time.

Part C · Timed scenario (45 minutes, 300–450 words)

The brief. A large public polyclinic group in Singapore wants a chatbot on its patient app that answers questions about the patient's own prescribed medicines: what each one is for, when to take it, common side effects, and what to do after a missed dose. It would draw on the patient's prescription record and the group's approved medicine leaflets. The chief executive wants a launch in six months and has asked you for a recommendation.

Write a memo that covers: (1) whether to build it, in full, in part or not at all, and why; (2) the smallest first version you would launch; (3) three things it must never do; (4) how you would know before launch that it is safe and useful; (5) the one risk you are least sure how to handle.

Set a timer and stop at 45 minutes. Do not use AI tools or references. The point is your unaided starting judgement.

How Part C will be scored (0–4 on each)

  • Decision quality: a clear recommendation, with the alternatives you rejected
  • Evidence: what you would need to know, and which statements are assumptions
  • Risk handling: failure modes, who is harmed, and how each is controlled
  • Communication: a chief executive could act on it after a five-minute read
