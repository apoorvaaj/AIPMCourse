Designing AI user experience and human factors

An artificial intelligence (AI) product is only as safe as what people do with its answers. Design what people see when the system is wrong, unsure or out of its depth, so that their trust matches how reliable it really is.

In Modules 4 to 6 you specified Harbourview's medicine questions assistant, found its failures and built a release gate. Every one of those checks looked at the answer. None of them looked at the person reading it. When the patient panel sat eight patients and carers in front of the assistant, they found problems no evaluation had caught: a patient who thought the assistant was a pharmacist, a carer who ignored an emergency message, a woman who gave up because a leaflet was in English only. The answers were right. What people did with them was not.

This module is about that gap. In product teams it is called user experience (UX) design for AI, and the study of how people actually think and behave around automated systems is called human factors. The module draws on the third course of Duke University's AI Product Management specialisation (Human Factors in AI), the Google People + AI Research (PAIR) Guidebook, Microsoft Research's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction, and decades of research on trust in automation from aviation and medicine.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  1. Design for trust that matches how reliable the system is: set expectations, and show sources and uncertainty in ways people understand.
  2. Design what happens when the AI is wrong, unsure or out of scope, including emergencies, refusals and handovers to a person, with the exact words people see.
  3. Choose the right level of human review for each AI action, and design against automation bias.

Step 1 · Lesson · about 90 minutesDesigning for people who rely on systems that are sometimes wrong

Part 1 sets the goal: trust that matches reliability. Parts 2 and 3 cover setting expectations, explaining and showing uncertainty. Part 4 designs the moments when things go wrong. Part 5 decides how much a person should check before the AI acts. Part 6 covers people at the edges and how to test what you design.

7.1 The goal is calibrated trust, not maximum trust

Rule-based software does the same thing every time, so people learn quickly when to rely on it. AI products vary: the same question can get a different answer, and the product is right most of the time but not always. People still form a picture of what it can do, and they decide, often without noticing, how much to rely on it. Researchers who study automation, from aircraft autopilots to hospital alarms, call the right outcome calibrated trust: people rely on the system when it is reliable for the task in front of them, and check, question or bypass it when it is not.

People rely on itPeople check or bypass it System is right
Value gainedThe product does its job
DisuseCorrect help ignored; people call anyway
System is wrong
MisuseA wrong answer is acted on
Harm avoidedHealthy scepticism

The words misuse (relying on automation when you should not) and disuse (ignoring automation that would have helped) come from human factors research by Raja Parasuraman and Victor Riley in 1997. Both are product failures. Disuse wastes the money spent on the AI. Misuse causes harm. A design that maximises trust pushes people into the bottom-left box whenever the AI is wrong.

What moves trust without changing reliability

Raises trust, whether or not it is deserved

  • A human name, face or personality
  • Confident, fluent, friendly wording
  • Speed: instant answers feel authoritative
  • Citations that look official, even if they do not support the claim
  • Saying "I" as if a person is reading

Lowers trust, whether or not it is deserved

  • One visible mistake with no explanation or correction
  • Refusals that give no reason and no next step
  • Answers that change when you ask again
  • Long, hedged answers that bury the point
  • Technical error messages

The design job is to make trust signals honest: tie each one to something that is actually true about the system at that moment, such as a real source, a real scope rule or a real person who will reply.

7.2 Mental models and setting expectations

A mental model is the picture a person carries of how something works. People do not arrive at your product blank: they bring models from search engines ("type keywords"), from texting a friend ("it knows me"), and from talking to staff ("someone qualified is reading this"). If the model is wrong, even correct answers get misused. The cheapest time to shape the model is the first moment of use, and the most important times are the moments when the product reaches a limit.

Microsoft Research's 18 Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction (Saleema Amershi and colleagues, 2019) begin with two that matter most here: make clear what the system can do, and make clear how well it can do it. The PAIR Guidebook adds: explain the benefit, not the technology.

  • Say what it is. An automated assistant, not a person. Harbourview made this a requirement in Module 4 (R-10), but only "when asked". People who hold the wrong model rarely ask.
  • Say what it can and cannot do, at the moment it matters. A short line on first use, and again whenever someone reaches a limit. Nobody reads a terms page.
  • Say where the answers come from. "From your medicine leaflets" tells people both why to trust it and where its knowledge stops.
  • Show by example. Two or three suggested questions teach the scope faster than a description.
  • Say who is behind it. If a pharmacist or nurse can be reached, say how and when.

"Ask me anything" is a promise. Broad invitations set an expectation the product will break within a few messages. Each break costs more trust than the invitation gained.

7.3 Explanations and uncertainty

Two audiences, two kinds of explanation

For the person using it

What should I do, and why should I believe this? Answered by the source ("from your Metformin 500 mg leaflet, section 3"), the scope ("I only answer from your leaflets") and the reason for a limit ("pregnancy questions always go to a pharmacist"). Short, in plain words, at the point of decision.

For auditors and the product team

Why did the system do this? Answered by logs: which rule fired, which passage was retrieved, which model and prompt version, which judge result. Detailed, stored, never shown to the patient. This is what lets you answer a complaint or a regulator.

Explanations are not automatically good for users. Several studies of AI-assisted decisions have found that adding explanations made people accept the AI's wrong recommendations more often, because any explanation looks like evidence. A source citation helps calibration only if the source genuinely supports the claim, which is why Module 6's judge for unsupported claims matters to UX as well as to quality.

Showing uncertainty: three options

OptionExampleProblem or strength
A number"Confidence: 82%"Most people cannot act on it (is 82% good?). A language model's own statement of confidence is not a reliable measure of whether it is right. Use numbers only for expert users, and only when the number is measured.
Hedging words"This may possibly be…"Hedges in every answer make everything sound equally unsure, so people learn to ignore them.
A change in behaviourAsks a clarifying question; offers two choices; gives the part it is sure of and hands over the restUsually best The person does not have to interpret anything; the product simply does the safer thing. Drive it from real system signals, not from the model's words.

7.4 Designing the moments when things go wrong

Evaluation finds where an AI fails. UX design decides what the person experiences when it does. The PAIR Guidebook's chapter on errors starts from the user's view: from the outside, a system limitation, a misunderstanding, a wrong answer and a server failure can all look the same ("it didn't work"), and each needs a different path to recovery.

Map the moments before you write any words

For each product, list the moments where it reaches a limit. Most AI products have all of these:

Kind of momentWhat has happenedResponse ladder rung (Module 4)
EmergencyA message suggests someone may be in danger now5 · Emergency stop
HandoverA rule or a limit sends the question to a person3 or 4
UnsureThe request is ambiguous, or the system has more than one readingAsk, or offer choices
Out of scope or refusedThe product cannot or must not do it4 · Decline and hand over, or redirect
System failureA step failed: search, model, booking systemFallback
Wrong answer found laterAn audit or a complaint shows a past answer was wrongCorrection

Anatomy of a good message at a limit

  1. What happened, in plain words"I couldn't reach the leaflet library just now." No error codes, no blame on the person.
  2. Why, briefly, if it helps"Pregnancy questions always go to a pharmacist." One clause. A reason makes a limit feel deliberate rather than broken.
  3. What happens next, who, and when, honestlyName the person or team and a time that is true at this hour of the day.
  4. What the person can do nowOne clear action, as a button where possible, plus an escape route if things get worse.
  5. Keep their wordsNever make someone retype a question to a person. Carry the conversation over.

The emergency exception. For a possible emergency, only parts 3 and 4 matter, and part 4 comes first: "Call 995 now." Nothing to read before it, no question to answer first, no reason that could invite the person to argue themselves out of calling. Anything else, such as a quieter option for someone whose symptom has passed, goes underneath and must not compete.

Handovers deserve as much design as answers

A handover is the product admitting a person is needed. Done well, it builds trust. Done badly, it feels like being dropped. A warm handover carries the question and context to the person, tells the user who will reply and when, shows the status while they wait ("A pharmacist has your question"), and says what to do if things get worse in the meantime. If replies are slower at night, say so at night. A promise that is true at 2 pm and false at 10 pm teaches people that the product's words cannot be relied on.

Let people correct the AI, and make it count

Give people a way to say "that's not what I meant", to change a detail before an action happens, to undo an action, and to report a problem. Feedback controls only build trust if something visibly follows. A thumbs-down button whose meaning is unclear, or that changes nothing, is noise.

7.5 How much should a person check before the AI acts?

Some AI outputs are only words a person reads. Others cause something to happen: an appointment moves, a note enters a medical record, a message goes to a nurse. For each action, decide how much a person checks. A simple five-level scale, adapted from Thomas Sheridan's levels of automation:

  1. L0Person onlyThe AI is not used for this action at all.
  2. L1AI suggests, person decides and actsA draft or suggestion that a person can ignore. Example: suggested reply for a pharmacist.
  3. L2Preview, then approveThe AI prepares the action; nothing happens until a person confirms it. Example: "Move your review to Thu 14 Nov, 3:40 pm at Clementi? Confirm / Change".
  4. L3Act, then notify, with undoThe AI acts and tells the person, who can reverse it within a window.
  5. L4Act alone, auditedThe AI acts; people review a sample afterwards.

Choosing a level

Ask three questions about a wrong action: how bad is it (severity), can it be undone (reversibility), and would anyone notice in time (detectability)? Severe, irreversible, hard-to-spot actions need a person before the action (L0 to L2). Minor, reversible, obvious ones can run with an undo or an audit (L3 or L4). One exception: when speed is the safety, as with an emergency message, putting a person in front of it adds delay that causes harm. There, use a well-tested fixed rule that acts alone, and audit it.

Automation bias: why "a person approves it" is not enough

Automation bias is the tendency to accept an automated output without checking it properly. It grows as the system gets more reliable: when drafts are right 97 times in 100, reviewers stop looking hard, and the three wrong ones sail through. A preview-then-approve step can quietly become a rubber stamp. Designs that help:

  • Show only what needs checking, and highlight the risky parts: medicines, doses, allergies, dates, money.
  • Ask for confirmation of the specific risky item ("Confirm the medicine list"), not one "Approve all" button.
  • Make disagreeing as easy as agreeing. No pre-ticked boxes; editing is one tap away.
  • Feed back what audits find, so reviewers know their checks matter.
  • Watch the signal from Module 6: editing that falls while audited error rates stay flat means checking has dropped, not quality risen.

7.6 People at the edges, and testing what you design

Average users forgive a lot. The people most likely to be harmed by a confusing AI moment are the ones least represented in design reviews: older adults, carers acting for someone else, people writing in a second language or mixing languages, people with low vision or low literacy, and people who are frightened or in pain. Design for them first and most others benefit.

  • Short sentences, one action per message. Aim for words a 12-year-old would know. Long medical words cost trust and comprehension.
  • Large, labelled buttons instead of instructions to type a keyword. Do not rely on colour alone to carry meaning.
  • Follow the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 at level AA, the level most organisations and governments aim for: readable contrast, text that can be enlarged, everything usable with a screen reader.
  • Translate safety-critical fixed messages in advance, have them checked by qualified people, and store them. Do not let a model translate an emergency instruction on the fly.
  • Design for carers explicitly. People already act for parents and children; if the product has no honest way for them to do so, they borrow logins.

Test behaviour, not opinions

Asking "do you trust it?" tells you little. Calibration shows up in what people do. Give participants scenarios where the AI is right and scenarios where it is wrong or at a limit, then ask "what would you do now?" and "what happens next?". Record:

  • Did they act correctly when the AI was right? (disuse shows up here)
  • Did they catch it, or take the safe path, when the AI was wrong? (misuse shows up here)
  • Could they say who would reply and when, after a handover?
  • Who did they think they were talking to?
  • How long did it take to find the way to a person?

Five to eight participants from the edge groups find most serious comprehension problems. That is not a statistical test; it is a search for problems, like the error analysis you did in Module 5.

    End-of-module test · 25%

    • Format: 10 Yes/No questions (1 mark each) and 5 short-answer questions (up to 3 marks each). Total 25 marks.
    • Rules: closed book, 30 minutes, taken once. No notes, no AI tools.
    • Covers: everything in the lesson. Expect to rewrite a weak message, choose and justify review levels, critique a confidence display, design an emergency moment, and spot automation bias, for a product you have not seen.
    • Opens: once both checkpoint quizzes are at mastery. After emailing your assignment, email the course team to request the test paper and book your viva.

    Viva · 15%

    A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice") is a short spoken examination. Yours lasts 10 minutes, with an assessor or a trained peer.

    MinutesWhat happens
    0–2Without reading, you explain what patients should and should not rely on each product for, and how your design signals it.
    2–7Questions on your choices. Expect "defend every word of your chest pain frame", "why that review level for moving a review appointment?" and "what did you do about Ivy, and what would change your mind?"
    7–10The assessor gives you a new moment or a new finding. You design the words or the response on the spot, and say what it changes.

    It is marked on: whether you can defend your choices with evidence, whether you can explain the ideas in plain words, how you handle the new moment, and how clearly you speak. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well.

    Optional lab · about 1 hour · not assessedTest your storyboard with two people over 65, or carers

    Nothing replaces watching someone read your words. Find two people aged over 65, or two people who care for an older relative. Family, neighbours and friends are fine.

    Keep it safe and fictional. Tell each person the products and scenarios are made up for a course, and that you are testing your words, not them. Do not ask about their own health.     Going furtherReading and practice ideas

    Reading

    • Google People + AI Research, People + AI Guidebook: Mental Models, Explainability + Trust and Errors + Graceful Failure. Practical chapters with worksheets. About 30 minutes each.
    • Saleema Amershi and colleagues, Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction (Microsoft Research, 2019). Eighteen guidelines, each with examples. Read the list and two or three examples, about 20 minutes.
    • John Lee and Katrina See, Trust in automation: designing for appropriate reliance (Human Factors, 2004). The classic paper on calibrated trust. Read the introduction and conclusions, about 25 minutes.
    • Duke University, Human Factors in AI (Coursera). The course this module draws on; audit the modules on user experience and trust.
    • World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2. Skim the summaries for contrast, text size and labels.

    Practise on your own world

    • Card fraud alert for a bank's app: storyboard the "Was this you?" moment for a blocked payment, at 2 am, for a customer abroad. What happens if they tap "No"? What if they ignore it? Which actions can the system take alone?
    • Meeting notes assistant: it sends action items to attendees after each meeting. Choose a review level for sending, and design how a person corrects an action item that was never agreed.
    • Insurance claims chat: design the message a customer sees at 11 pm on a Saturday after their water-leak claim is handed to a person who works weekdays.
