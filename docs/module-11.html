Modules 1 to 10 looked at Harbourview's five artificial intelligence (AI) initiatives one at a time: whether each needs AI, how to specify, test, design, pay for and govern it. This module puts them together. It is early June. The board will spend 26 June on the AI portfolio, and the chief executive officer (CEO) wants to leave that day with an 18-month strategy he can defend to the Ministry of Health and to staff: which initiatives to build, buy, share with a partner or drop, in what order, within the money and people Harbourview has, and a six-week proof of concept (POC) plan for the first thing it does.

The module draws on Reforge's AI Strategy and AI Growth courses (defensibility when models are widely available), the strategy and go-to-market units of Marily Nika's AI Product Management Bootcamp, Kellogg Executive Education's AI-Driven Product Strategy programme (vision, portfolio, roadmapping and go-to-market), Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers, Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, and recent public evidence on how organisations buy, build and adopt AI, including the first randomised trials of AI note-taking tools in clinics.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  1. Decide whether to build, buy, partner on or drop each AI initiative, using total cost of ownership, capability, control and the cost of leaving, not the price tag alone.
  2. Tell a lasting advantage from a thin layer over someone else's model, and write a strategy that still holds after the next model release.
  3. Plan how a product reaches its users: a proof of concept with exit criteria agreed before it starts, a phased rollout with gates, and adoption measured by real use rather than licences sold.

Step 1 · Lesson · about 90 minutesChoices, durability and adoption

Part 1 says what an AI strategy is and is not. Part 2 asks what lasts when everyone can rent the same models. Part 3 covers build, buy or partner. Part 4 covers lock-in and keeping a way out. Part 5 covers proofs of concept that actually decide something. Part 6 covers rollout and adoption.

11.1 What an AI strategy is, and what it is not

Most documents called "AI strategy" are lists of hopes: "use AI to improve patient experience", "become an AI-first organisation". Richard Rumelt calls this bad strategy: goals dressed up as a plan. A good strategy has three parts, which he calls the kernel:

  1. 1DiagnosisWhat is actually going on? The few facts that matter most, such as "doctors spend a quarter of each consultation typing" or "our phone line loses one booking call in five".
  2. 2Guiding policyThe overall approach that follows from the diagnosis, including what it rules out. For example: "rent models, own our tests, content and workflow; buy where the job is the same as everyone else's".
  3. 3Coherent actionsA short list of things you will do, in an order, with the money and people you have, that reinforce each other.

For an AI portfolio, that turns into five questions. Where will AI make the biggest difference to the people we serve, and where will it not? For each initiative, do we build, buy, partner or drop? What will we own whatever happens to the models? In what order, given our money and our people? How will we know it is working, and what will make us stop?

Two habits separate a strategy from a wish list. First, a list of what we will not do, at least for now: if nothing is dropped or deferred, nothing was decided. Second, sequencing against capacity. Money is often not the binding limit; the small number of people who can build, test and run AI products usually is. Every live product keeps using some of them (Modules 5, 6 and 10: evaluations, monitoring, red-teams, model upgrades).

Portfolio
The set of initiatives taken together, so that you can balance quick wins against bigger bets and see what competes for the same money and people.
Capacity
How much work your team can actually do in a period, after the products already live have taken their share.
Total cost of ownership (TCO)
Everything an option costs over a fixed period (usually three years): one-off costs, recurring fees, your own people's time to run it, price rises, and what it would cost to leave.
Proof of concept (POC)
A short, limited trial that answers one question well enough to make a decision.
Pilot
A longer trial in real service with a limited group, to see whether value and safety hold in daily use.
Rollout
Extending a product to everyone it is meant for, usually in waves with checks between them.

Strategy for a public provider. Harbourview does not compete for profit, so "defensibility" means something slightly different. A company asks "can a competitor copy this?". A public provider asks "will this investment still be worth it after the next model release, and can we change supplier without starting again?" The tools are the same; the question is durability and independence.

11.2 What lasts when everyone can rent the same model

The model layer is widely available and keeps getting cheaper for the same capability (Module 9). A feature that is essentially "a prompt over a model" is a thin layer: easy to copy, and often absorbed into the next release of general-purpose assistants. What lasts sits around the model: the data and context only you have, the place the product holds in people's daily work, the trust you have earned, and the tests that let you improve or swap the model safely.

Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers lists the seven sources of lasting advantage in business. Each has an AI version, and each has a trap.

Data is an advantage only if it is unique, you have the right to use it, it is relevant to the job, it keeps improving with use, and you actually turn it into better behaviour through your tests. Reforge's AI Growth course frames the same idea for companies: growth comes from acquiring, keeping and earning from users, multiplied by defensibility, and the defensibility factors to check are proprietary data, network effects and switching costs.

The next model release test

Imagine a model ships tomorrow that is twice as good at half the price. Go through each part of your product and ask: does it get better (the new model is a tailwind) or does it become unnecessary (a headwind: someone else now does it for free)? Put your own money and people into the tailwind parts, and rent the rest.

11.3 Build, buy or partner

Every initiative has four routes: build (your team builds it, usually on a rented model), buy (license a vendor's product), partner (share or co-develop: a shared platform, a consortium, a vendor you co-design with) or drop (not now). Few answers are pure; most products are bought or shared at the core with a thin layer of your own on top.

QuestionPoints to buildPoints to buy or partner
Is this where we differ?It is part of what makes you you, and the job is specific to youThe job is the same as everyone else's (note-taking, booking, text analysis)
Does it need data or context only we have?Yes, and a vendor could not get it or use it as wellThe vendor can be given the context through integration

What must we control?Safety rules, data, the roadmap or regulatory evidence that a vendor will not let you controlA contract can give you enough control: audit rights, data terms, change notice
What would it cost to leave?Leaving a vendor later would be very expensiveYou can keep the exit open (Part 11.4)

Total cost of ownership

Compare options over the same period, usually three years, and include: one-off costs (integration, templates, data preparation, training, governance work); recurring costs (licence or usage fees, hosting, monitoring, human review and audit time); your own people's time to run, test and upgrade it, priced at what that time is worth; change costs (price rises at renewal, re-testing after model changes); and the cost to leave. Then put value on the same timeline: a cheaper option that goes live nine months later can be the expensive one.

What the evidence says (real studies)

  • Most organisations now buy. Menlo Ventures' 2025 survey of about 500 United States enterprise decision-makers found the share of AI use cases bought from vendors rose from roughly half in 2024 to about three-quarters in 2025. A widely reported 2025 study from MIT's NANDA initiative found that tools bought from specialised vendors or developed in partnership reached production success far more often than internal builds. Both are United States studies and the MIT report's methods and definition of success have been questioned, so treat them as a signal, not a rule.
  • "AI scribe" is a category, not a result. A randomised trial at UCLA Health, published in NEJM AI in late 2025, assigned 238 outpatient doctors to one of two commercial AI scribes or to usual practice. One product reduced time spent writing notes by about a tenth compared with usual practice; the other showed no significant change. Both improved measures of burnout, and the authors reported occasional clinically significant errors. Two products, same doctors, same weeks, different results: test the specific product in your setting.
  • Vendors change. The Permanente Medical Group (part of Kaiser Permanente) reported in NEJM Catalyst that 7,260 of its doctors used an AI scribe in about 2.5 million patient visits between October 2023 and December 2024. It changed vendor between its 10-week pilot and the full rollout, and uptake varied a lot between specialties.
  • Partnering through a shared platform. In Singapore, SingHealth's own digital team built Note Buddy, an AI scribe that works in English, Mandarin, Malay and Tamil, on Tandem, the secure generative AI platform of Synapxe, the national health technology agency. It launched in 2024 and has since been extended to other public healthcare institutions: one organisation builds, a shared platform lets others use it.

11.4 Lock-in, and keeping the way out open

Every route creates dependence. The question is whether you can still leave at a cost you can bear. There are five kinds of lock-in:

  • Model lock-in: prompts and behaviour tuned to one model, with no tests to show whether another would do.
  • Product lock-in: staff work inside a vendor's templates and screens.
  • Data lock-in: transcripts, labels or logs you cannot export in a usable form.
  • Contract lock-in: multi-year minimums, automatic renewal, price rises at the vendor's discretion.
  • Skills lock-in: only the vendor understands how the product is set up.

Habits that keep the exit open:

  1. Own your tests. Your test sets, judges and red-team cases (Modules 5, 6 and 10) let you check a replacement in days instead of months. This is the single best protection.
  2. Keep your content and configuration in your own hands: prompts, approved content, templates, rules.
  3. Put a thin layer between your product and the model (often called a gateway), so that switching model is a configuration change plus a test run.
  4. Write the exit into the contract before you sign: data export in a standard format at no extra fee; no use of your data for training unless you opt in; a cap on price rises at renewal; advance notice of model changes, so your change control (Module 10) can run; audit rights and a model card; help with leaving and a reasonable notice period; termination for safety breaches; service levels with remedies.
  5. Be wary of large commitments in exchange for discounts. A 20% discount for a three-year commitment on every seat is only a saving if the seats are used (Module 9).

Pilot purgatory is what happens when a trial never ends because nobody agreed what success would look like: it runs on, users lose interest, and the decision is made by default. The cure is to write the decision down before the trial starts.

A POC that decides something has

  • One decision it informs, and the person who will make it, on a date.
  • A hypothesis with numbers: "the scribe saves at least one minute per consultation for doctors who did not volunteer".
  • Representative users, not only volunteers. Volunteers are early adopters; their results overstate what everyone else will see (Module 9).
  • A baseline measured the same way, before or alongside.
  • Measures with thresholds: the main benefit, safety, real use, user experience and cost.
  • Exit criteria for go, extend and stop, signed before the start, plus a safety stop rule that ends it at once.
  • Enough time to get past novelty: first-week enthusiasm is not habit.
  • A cost cap and an honest list of what the POC cannot tell you.

When two products compete, run a head-to-head comparison: the same kind of users, the same kind of work, the same measures, at the same time. Either split users into matched groups, or give each user both products in a random order.

11.6 Rollout and adoption

Licences are not adoption. Value comes from people using the product for most of the work it is meant for, week after week. Follow people down an adoption funnel:

  1. 1AccessThey have it: a licence, an account, the app.
  2. 2ActivationThey have used it for real at least once.
  3. 3Regular useThey use it every week.
  4. 4DepthThey use it for most of the tasks it is meant for, not just the easy ones.
  5. 5OutcomeThe benefit appears: minutes saved, calls avoided, patients helped, and it is turned into something real (Module 9's "realisation").

Who adopts first. Everett Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations describes adopters in five groups: innovators (about 2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%) and laggards (16%). The early groups put up with rough edges because they enjoy new tools. The majority adopt when the product fits their work and colleagues they respect use it. A rollout plan has to win the majority, not just delight the volunteers.

What drives adoption in practice. Fit with the existing workflow (templates that match the work, fewer clicks, nothing to copy across); a visible benefit in the first week; training at the moment of use, not in a lecture; respected champions in each team; a feedback channel that leads to visible fixes; leaders who use it themselves; and keeping the old way available until the new one works. The change-management model ADKAR (Prosci) checks the same things from the person's side: Awareness of why, Desire to take part, Knowledge of how, Ability in practice, and Reinforcement so it sticks.

Phased rollout. Roll out in waves (sites, teams or user groups), with a gate between waves: quality and safety still within thresholds, adoption at or above target, enough capacity for the work it creates (Module 9's pharmacist queue), governance approval for the next step (Module 10), and a way to roll back. Kaiser's experience above is typical: uptake varies by team, and teams that adapted templates to their own work adopted more.

              Viva · 15%

              A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice") is a short spoken examination. Yours lasts 10 minutes, with an assessor or a trained peer.

              MinutesWhat happens
              0–2Without reading, you give the board your strategy in a few sentences: what first, what not at all, and why.
              2–7Questions on your strategy. Expect "what will Harbourview own whatever happens?", "why that route for the note drafter?" and "what would make you stop the POC?"
              7–10The assessor changes one fact: a vendor's price, a new model, a failed gate, a budget cut. You say on the spot what it does to your plan.

              It is marked on: whether you can defend your choices with evidence, whether you can explain the ideas in plain words, how you handle the changed fact, and how clearly you speak. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well.

              Going furtherReading and practice ideas

              Reading

              • Richard Rumelt, Good Strategy/Bad Strategy (2011), chapters 1–5: the kernel, and how to recognise bad strategy. About 90 minutes.
              • Hamilton Helmer, 7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy (2016). Read the introduction and the chapters on switching costs, cornered resource and process power.
              • Menlo Ventures, 2025: The State of Generative AI in the Enterprise (December 2025). The build-versus-buy section; about 15 minutes.
              • Fortune, MIT report: 95% of generative AI pilots at companies are failing (August 2025). Read it alongside its critics; ask what "failing" was measured against.
              • UCLA Health, UCLA study finds AI scribes may reduce documentation time, summarising Lukac and colleagues, "Ambient AI Scribes in Clinical Practice: A Randomized Trial", NEJM AI (2025).
              • Kaiser Permanente Division of Research, AI-assisted notetaking gains steady support from Kaiser Permanente physicians, summarising the one-year review in NEJM Catalyst (2025).
              • SingHealth, SingHealth implements Note Buddy (September 2024). A Singapore example of building on a shared public platform.
              • Brian Balfour, AI Growth (Reforge course outline): defensibility factors in AI markets.
              • Kellogg Executive Education, AI-Driven Product Strategy: the programme outline is a useful map of portfolio, roadmapping and go-to-market topics.
              • Prosci, the ADKAR model, for planning adoption from the individual's side.

              Practise on your own world

              • Run the next model release test on a product you work on or use every day. Which parts get better with a stronger model, and which disappear?
              • Read one contract or terms of service for an AI tool your organisation uses. Can you export your data? Who decides when the model changes? What happens to the price at renewal?
              • Find a stalled pilot in your organisation. Write the exit criteria it should have started with, and decide what they say now.
              • Product idea: a simple "adoption funnel" dashboard for any internal tool, fed by its usage logs: licences, activation, weekly use, depth. Most organisations do not have one.
