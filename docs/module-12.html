Eleven modules taught you to frame, specify, test, design, price, govern and plan an AI product. The capstone asks you to do all of it for one real decision, and then to stand in front of the people who would carry the consequences.

Every module so far ended with a decision about part of a product. The capstone ends with the whole decision: should this AI product be launched, launched in a limited way, held back until something is true, or not launched at all? You choose one of Harbourview's five initiatives (or a product of your own, with the course team's agreement), assemble a capstone pack from your module work and the new evidence in this week's case, and defend your recommendation to a panel of three executives: the chief financial officer (CFO) on cost, the chief medical officer (CMO) on patient safety, and the chief information officer (CIO) on reliability and data.

The capstone follows the pattern of the capstones in leading public courses, such as the capstone and Demo Day in Marily Nika's AI Product Management Bootcamp, where learners build and pitch a product, and adds what those rarely include: a decision with conditions, a check that the evidence holds together, and a cross-examination by the people who would carry the risk. It also draws on Amazon's "working backwards" habits, Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle for putting the answer first, and Gary Klein's pre-mortem.

By the end of the capstone you will be able to

  1. Make a launch decision for an AI product, choosing between launch, limited launch, not yet and do not launch, and state the conditions, stop rules and monitoring that come with it.
  2. Assemble evidence from many sources into one consistent argument: find where earlier work contradicts new facts, say how strong each piece of evidence is, and say what is still assumed.
  3. Defend the decision to executives with different concerns, answer challenges you did not prepare for, and say plainly what you do not know.

Week 12 · Step 1 · Lesson · about 75 minutesMaking and defending a launch decision

Part 1 sets out the four honest answers to "should we launch?". Part 2 turns a pile of module deliverables into one argument. Part 3 sets the evidence bar and shows what happens when it is set too low. Part 4 covers launch criteria, stop rules and the pre-mortem. Part 5 covers the panel. Part 6 explains why you will write your Week 0 memo again.

12.1 Four honest answers to "should we launch?"

A launch decision is rarely yes or no. There are four answers, and choosing between them is most of the skill.

  1. 1LaunchRelease to everyone the product is meant for, with monitoring and stop rules. The evidence is strong on every dimension that could cause serious harm.
  2. 2Limited launchRelease to a defined group, place, language or set of tasks, with named conditions for going further. The evidence is strong for that scope and weaker outside it.
  3. 3Not yetDo not release now, because something specific must first be true: a fix retested, a contract term agreed, a staff rota in place. Name the condition and the date you will look again.
  4. 4Do not launchStop, because the value is not there, the risk cannot be brought down to an acceptable level, or something cheaper does the job. Say what you learned and what the money and people will do instead.

Limited launch is not a compromise. It is the right answer when the evidence really differs by scope: strong in English, thin in Tamil; strong for routine visits, weak for chronic disease reviews. It becomes an evasion when the limits are vague ("a soft launch"), when there is no condition for going further, or when the limited group is chosen to make the results look good.

"Do not launch" is not a failure. A product manager who stops a weak product early saves the money, people and trust it would have consumed. What fails is stopping without saying why, or launching anyway because work has already been done. Money already spent and not recoverable (a sunk cost) is not a reason to continue.

Launch decision
A commitment to release a product to a defined scope, with conditions, owners, monitoring and stop rules.
Scope
Who gets the product, where, in which languages, for which tasks, and from when.
Condition
Something that must be true before the launch goes ahead or expands, stated so that anyone can check it.
Stop rule
A measured result that ends or rolls back the launch at once, set in advance with a named person who can call it.
Rollback
Returning to the previous way of working (or the previous version) quickly and safely.

How much evidence? It depends on the door

Jeff Bezos, in his 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders, separated decisions that are hard or impossible to reverse ("one-way doors") from decisions that can be undone cheaply ("two-way doors"). Two-way doors should be taken quickly, by small groups; one-way doors deserve slow, careful work. Most AI launches are a mixture: switching a feature off is usually easy, but a three-year contract, a staff rota that has been cut, a lost patient's trust or a wrong entry in a medical record are not. Find the one-way parts of your decision and put most of your evidence there.

12.2 One argument, not a scrapbook

By Week 12 you have eleven deliverables. Stapling them together is not a capstone. A panel needs one argument: a decision, the few reasons that carry it, and the evidence under each reason. Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle, written for consultants at McKinsey, gives the shape: put the answer first, then the reasons, then the evidence.

The decision · one or two sentences, with scope and conditions
Three reasons · each one a claim the panel could disagree with
Evidence under each reason · figures, test results, user findings, costs, with where they came from and how strong they are

Amazon's "working backwards" habit makes the same point from the user's side. Before building, teams write the press release and the list of frequently asked questions (the "PR/FAQ") for the finished product, so the argument for it is clear before any work is done. For a launch decision, ask: what would we tell patients and staff on the day we launch, and could we say it truthfully?

Check that it holds together

Work done over twelve weeks drifts. Figures change, assumptions are replaced by measurements, and the facts you used in Week 4 may not be the facts in Week 12. A panel will find the contradiction you missed. Before you write, do a consistency audit: list every figure and claim your recommendation depends on, find its source, and check it against the newest evidence. These are the contradictions that turn up most often:

  • Old numbers: a cost model built on volunteer results when the trial has since measured everyone else.
  • Changed thresholds: a requirement says one thing, the release gate checks another.
  • Different scope: the requirements document covers four languages, the test set covers two.
  • Review levels that moved: the design says a pharmacist checks every dose answer; the cost model assumes they check 2%.
  • Decisions overtaken: your strategy chose one route, the board chose another.
  • Risk level mismatch: the trust pack says the product is low risk, the launch plan uses it for clinical advice.

When you find one, do not quietly keep the more convenient version. Say which you now rely on and why, and update the earlier work. A short change log in the pack shows the panel you have done this.

12.3 The evidence bar

Every launch rests on six kinds of evidence. For each, ask what you know, how strongly, and what you are assuming.

DimensionThe questionStrong evidence looks likeWeak evidence looks like

Label each piece of evidence strong, moderate, weak or assumption. A recommendation can rest on assumptions, as long as they are labelled, do not concern serious harm, and are checked soon after launch.

What the evidence says (real studies)

  • A widely used model, checked independently, did far worse than claimed. In 2021 researchers at Michigan Medicine published an external check of the Epic Sepsis Model, a sepsis warning tool built into a medical record system used by hundreds of hospitals (Wong and colleagues, JAMA Internal Medicine). Its ability to rank patients by risk was much lower than the developer had reported. At the alert threshold it missed about two-thirds of the patients who developed sepsis, while generating alerts on about one in five of all hospitalised patients. Evidence from the people who built a product is not the same as evidence from your setting.
  • Accurate in the lab, struggling in the clinic. Google's deep learning system for spotting diabetic eye disease performed at specialist level on test images. When researchers watched it in use at clinics in Thailand (Beede and colleagues, presented at the CHI conference on human factors in computing in 2020), many photos taken in real lighting conditions were rejected as too poor to grade, slow internet connections held up results, and nurses had to change how they worked. The model was the same; the setting was not. Launch evidence has to come from the place, people and workflow where the product will run.

12.4 Launch criteria, stop rules and the pre-mortem

A recommendation becomes a decision when it says how you will know it is working and what you will do if it is not. Write these before launch, as you did for the proof of concept (POC) in Module 11.

  • Conditions before launch: what must be true on the day. "Build 1.2.1 passes the release gate; Malay-speaking pharmacist cover is on the rota."
  • Measures after launch, each with a threshold, how it is measured, an owner and the action if it is crossed. Include at least one measure of value, one of safety, one of real use and one of the work the product creates for people.
  • Stop and rollback rules: the result that ends it, who can call it, and how quickly you can return to the old way. Test the rollback before you need it.
  • A review date, usually 30, 60 or 90 days after launch, when the decision is looked at again with real data.

The pre-mortem

Gary Klein's pre-mortem, described in the Harvard Business Review in 2007, reverses the usual post-mortem. Before the launch, the team imagines it is six months later and the launch has failed badly. Each person writes down, independently, why it failed. Klein drew on research by Deborah Mitchell, Jay Russo and Nancy Pennington (1989), which found that imagining an outcome had already happened made people better at explaining why it might happen. A pre-mortem makes it safe to voice doubts that people keep to themselves when everyone is enthusiastic.

12.5 Defending to a panel

A panel is not an audience. Each member is listening for the risk they will carry. Prepare for each of them separately.

Chief financial officerCOST AND COMMITMENT
What does it cost over its life? What value is real money and what is "time freed"? What does it commit us to beyond this budget, and what if adoption is half what you expect?
Chief medical officerPATIENT SAFETY
How often is it wrong, and how badly? Who checks it, and will they keep checking? Which patients are least well served? What stops it if something goes wrong?
Chief information officerRELIABILITY AND DATA
Where does the data go? What happens when the supplier or model changes? Can we test a new version, switch it off, and leave?

The shape of a 20-minute defence

MinutesWhat you cover
0–2The decision, its scope and conditions, in plain words. No background first.
2–12Three reasons, each with its strongest evidence and its weakest point.
12–16What could go wrong (from the pre-mortem), and how you will know and stop.
16–19Cost, commitment, and what you are not doing.
19–20What you are asking the panel to decide today.

Answering hard questions

  • Answer first, then the evidence, then its limit. "No. The trial measured 1.1 minutes for doctors who did not volunteer. That is six weeks at two clinics, so I would check it again at 90 days."
  • Say what you do not know, and how you would find out and by when. Panels forgive gaps; they do not forgive bluffing.
  • Take the other side seriously. Before the panel, write the best case against your recommendation (sometimes called steelmanning). If you cannot, you do not yet understand the decision.
  • Change your mind for a good reason, out loud. If a question reveals a flaw, say so and say what you would now recommend. That scores better than defending the indefensible.

Week 13 · Step 3 · Capstone pack · 8–10 hours · Graded 60%The capstone pack

Your task. Prepare the papers for the Launch Review on one initiative: a recommendation of about two pages, supported by an evidence map, a change log, launch criteria with stop rules, and a pre-mortem, plus the slides for your defence. The six tools below save as you go and export with your submission. Email the pack by the end of Week 13.

What your pack must contain

  1. Recommendation (about 900 words, two pages): the decision first, with scope and conditions; three reasons with evidence; what you are not doing; risks accepted and who owns them; cost and commitment; what you ask the panel to decide.
  2. Evidence map (Tool 2): six dimensions, each with the claim, the evidence and its source, its strength, and what is missing.
  3. Change log (Tool 3): at least three places where new facts changed or contradicted your earlier work, and what you did about each.
  4. Launch criteria, stop rules and monitoring (Tool 4), with a pre-mortem of at least five reasons and the control for each.
  5. Defence slides: no more than ten (Tool 6 gives the outline).
  6. Appendices: the earlier module deliverables you rely on, revised where the change log says so.

How it is marked

Each criterion is scored 0–4 using the programme rubric. A 0 or 1 on any criterion means a rewrite before grading.

CriterionFor a Distinction in the capstone
Decision qualityA clear launch, limited launch, not yet or do not launch, with scope, conditions, owners and a review date. Alternatives considered and the reasons they lost. One-way parts of the decision identified and treated more carefully. Fits the board's decision and the committee's conditions, or says where and why it differs.
EvidenceAll six dimensions covered, each labelled by strength, with sources from the modules and the September case. Figures recomputed from the newest evidence, with likely ranges where counts are small. The change log shows earlier work was checked and corrected. Assumptions are labelled and none concern serious harm without a control.
Risk handlingLaunch criteria with thresholds, owners and actions; a stop rule someone can call; a rollback that is planned. The pre-mortem finds the risks the panel would raise, including the people least well served, and each has a control. Commitments beyond the budget period are named.
CommunicationThe decision is in the first two sentences. The CFO, CMO and CIO can each find what they care about in two minutes. About two pages plus the map, log and criteria; ten slides or fewer; every acronym spelled out once.

            Before you submit the pack

            Week 14 · Step 4 · Close · 3–4 hours · Graded 40%Test, panel defence and the Week 0 memo

            Cumulative test · 25%

            • Format: 10 Yes/No questions (1 mark each) and 5 short-answer questions (up to 3 marks each), built around one new scenario. Total 25 marks.
            • Rules: closed book, 30 minutes, taken once. A basic calculator is allowed. No notes, no AI tools.
            • Covers: the whole programme. Expect to decide whether a problem needs AI, choose a design, read an error analysis and a judge's agreement figures, set a release gate, choose a level of human review, work out three-year cost and value, classify risk and fairness, choose build, buy or partner, and make a launch decision with stop rules.
            • Opens: once both checkpoint quizzes are at mastery and your pack has been emailed. Ask the course team for the paper.

            Panel defence · 15%

            The defence is the capstone's viva (viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice"): a spoken examination. It is longer than the usual 10 minutes because it tests the whole programme.

            MinutesWhat happens
            0–20Your presentation, ten slides or fewer. The panel does not interrupt.
            20–35Questions from the three roles: cost, patient safety, reliability and data. An assessor may play more than one role.
            35–40The panel changes one fact. You say on the spot what it does to your recommendation.

            It is marked on: whether you defend your choices with evidence, whether you can explain the ideas from any module in plain words, how you handle the changed fact, and how clearly you present and answer. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well. A passed defence (at least 50%) is required to complete the programme.

            The Week 0 memo, again · required, not graded

            Do this after your defence, in one sitting. Set a timer for 45 minutes, use no notes, references or AI tools, and write 300 to 450 words. The brief is exactly the one you answered in Week 0.

            The brief. A large public polyclinic group in Singapore wants a chatbot on its patient app that answers questions about the patient's own prescribed medicines: what each one is for, when to take it, common side effects, and what to do after a missed dose. It would draw on the patient's prescription record and the group's approved medicine leaflets. The chief executive wants a launch in six months and has asked you for a recommendation.

            Write a memo that covers: (1) whether to build it, in full, in part or not at all, and why; (2) the smallest first version you would launch; (3) three things it must never do; (4) how you would know before launch that it is safe and useful; (5) the one risk you are least sure how to handle.

            Optional lab · 3–6 hours · not assessedBuild a working slice and show it

            Capstones in courses such as Marily Nika's end with a working product. You can add one here: a thin, working version of one part of your chosen initiative, shown in no more than three minutes of your 20. It is not marked, but a working slice often answers questions the panel would otherwise ask.

            • Choose the riskiest assumption from your evidence map and build only what tests it: for example a retrieval demo over five invented leaflets with your handover rules (A), a clickable rescheduling flow with a "talk to a person" route (B), a note template for chronic disease reviews run on an invented transcript (C), a reminder message in four languages read aloud by a speaker of each (D), or a serious-complaint flag run on 50 invented comments including sarcastic ones (E).
            • Use no-code or low-code tools you are allowed to use, and invented data only. Never use real patient information.
            • Test it the way the programme taught you: write the expected behaviour first, run each case more than once, and record failures.

            Going furtherReading and practice ideas

            Reading

            • Gary Klein, Performing a project premortem, Harvard Business Review (September 2007). A page; about 10 minutes.
            • Andrew Wong and colleagues, External validation of a widely implemented proprietary sepsis prediction model in hospitalized patients, JAMA Internal Medicine (2021). Read the key points and the discussion.
            • Emma Beede and colleagues, A human-centered evaluation of a deep learning system deployed in clinics for the detection of diabetic retinopathy (2020), and Google's short summary.
            • Barbara Minto, The Pyramid Principle: the first two chapters, on putting the answer first.
            • Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, Working Backwards (2021): the chapter on the PR/FAQ.
            • Jeff Bezos, 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders: the passage on one-way and two-way doors.
            • Marily Nika, AI Product Management Bootcamp: the capstone and Demo Day outline, for how others structure a final project.

            Practise on your own world

            • Run a pre-mortem at work before your next launch or big decision. Ask everyone to write reasons alone, before anyone speaks.
            • Write the four answers (launch, limited, not yet, do not launch) for a product decision you are facing, with one sentence each on what would have to be true.
            • Find the one-way door in a decision that is being rushed, and ask for evidence there only.
            • Product idea: a "launch readiness review" tool that pulls a product's release gate results, adoption figures, cost and governance status into one page with the six dimensions and their strength. Most organisations assemble this by hand, late, for every launch.
