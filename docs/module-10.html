Responsible AI, risk and governance

Before an artificial intelligence (AI) product reaches more patients, someone outside the team will ask three questions: how risky is it, how did you try to break it, and who does it treat worse? Learn to answer them with evidence, and to write the documents that carry the answers.

Modules 4 to 9 asked whether Harbourview's AI products work, whether people can use them safely, and whether they are worth the money. This module asks whether they can be trusted by people outside the team: a governance committee, a regulator, a patient group. It is late April. Singapore's Ministry of Health (MOH) and Health Sciences Authority (HSA) have published new guidelines on AI in healthcare. Harbourview's new AI Governance Committee wants a trust pack before the medicine questions assistant reaches more clinics, and before Operations switches on the missed-appointment predictor.

You will classify both products' risk under the frameworks that apply, plan and record a structured attack test (a red-team) on the assistant, check the predictor for unfair effects on patient groups, write a model card, and brief the Ministry on one page. The module draws on Duke University's Human Factors in AI course (privacy, bias and ethics), the ethics and governance units of Marily Nika's Advanced AI Product Management, Product School's AI certification, and the frameworks themselves: MOH and HSA's Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare Guidelines (2026), the Infocomm Media Development Authority's (IMDA) governance frameworks and testing kits, the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework, the international AI management standard ISO/IEC 42001, and the European Union (EU) AI Act.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  1. Classify an AI product's risk under the frameworks that apply to it, say what each classification requires, and explain when your own harm analysis should set a higher bar.
  2. Test for harm before launch: plan, run and record a red-team, check a predictive model for unfair effects on groups, and turn findings into fixes, release rules and monitoring.
  3. Produce the governance documents a regulator or buyer will ask for: a risk classification, a red-team log, a model card, a fairness check and a one-page brief.

10.1 What responsible AI means for a product manager

Every framework in this module starts from principles: safety, fairness, transparency, accountability and a few more. Principles do not ship. A product manager (PM) turns each one into a decision, a requirement and a piece of evidence. You have done much of this already: the "must never" rules in Module 4, error analysis in Module 5, the release gate in Module 6, handovers in Module 7, agent limits in Module 8. Governance is the discipline of writing it down so that someone who was not in the room can check it.

The first six rows follow the seven ethical principles in MOH and HSA's 2026 guidelines (the seventh there is alignment with human values: the AI supports, and never replaces, professional judgment). Other frameworks use different words for the same ideas.

Harm
Damage to a person's health, safety, money, rights or dignity, or to an organisation. Always ask: harm to whom?
Risk
How bad a harm would be (severity) combined with how likely it is to happen (likelihood). A rare but catastrophic failure can be a higher risk than a common, trivial one.
Control
Something that lowers severity or likelihood: a rule, a test, a person checking, a limit, a contract term.
Residual risk
The risk left after controls. Someone with authority must accept it, in writing.
Governance
The people, processes and records that decide whether an AI product may be used, keep watch on it, and can stop it.

Who does what. MOH and HSA's guidelines split responsibility three ways: the developer who builds the AI, the deployer (the healthcare organisation that puts it into service) and the user (the healthcare professional who relies on it). When Harbourview builds its own product, it is both developer and deployer. The guidelines also recommend keeping three functions apart inside the deployer: a governance committee that approves, a unit that runs and monitors day to day, and a unit that audits. If you have worked in banking, you will recognise the "three lines of defence".

10.2 The frameworks, and which ones are law

A PM does not need to be a lawyer, but does need to know which documents are binding, which are voluntary, and which a buyer or regulator will use as a checklist. Here are the ones that matter for a Singapore healthcare product, plus two international references.

EU AI Act dates after the 2026 amendment. Prohibited practices have applied since February 2025 and duties for general-purpose AI models since August 2025. Most transparency duties, including telling people they are talking to an AI, applied from 2 August 2026. The "Digital Omnibus" amendment, in force since 27 July 2026, moved the high-risk duties to 2 December 2027 for the use cases listed in the Act's Annex III and to 2 August 2028 for AI inside products such as medical devices (Annex I). The duties were delayed, not removed.

Map the frameworks onto your work. NIST's four functions are a convenient way to see the trust pack. Govern: who decides and owns (committee, registry, roles). Map: what the system is and what could go wrong (classification, intended use). Measure: test it (evaluations, red-team, fairness check). Manage: act on what you find (fixes, release rules, monitoring, incidents).

10.3 Classifying risk

Classification decides which rules apply and how much evidence you owe. Work through four questions, in this order, and write down the reason for each answer.

  1. 1Is it a medical device?Read the intended use. Software intended for diagnosis, monitoring, treatment or alleviation of a condition is regulated by HSA. AIHGle 2.0 gives examples: an LLM that explains the side effects of medicines already prescribed to a patient is not a medical device; one that recommends medication combinations or dose adjustments is. Changing the intended use can change the answer.
  2. 2Which use category?AIHGle sorts healthcare AI into Clinical (supports clinical decisions), Clinical-Ops (part of a clinical workflow without directly making the clinical decision, such as note drafting or standardised medicine education) and Ops (operations such as scheduling). Ops falls outside AIHGle's focus; IMDA's frameworks apply instead.
  3. 3How much human oversight?Human-in-the-loop: nothing happens without a person's say-so. Human-over-the-loop: a person monitors and can take over. Human-out-of-the-loop: the AI acts alone. AIHGle's risk table combines this with the use category; the calculator below reproduces it.
  4. 4Cross-check against other frameworks.EU AI Act tier as a second opinion; data protection; whether patients use it directly; then your own internal tier, set by the harm you can foresee.
  5. 5Set the controls the classification requires, and any extra the harm requires.A classification is a floor. If your harm analysis finds something the framework did not anticipate, such as a score that falls harder on one group, your controls should go above the floor.

10.4 Red-teaming: trying to break it on purpose

A red-team is a structured attempt to make a system fail, by people thinking like attackers, careless users and people in distress. Your evaluations in Modules 5 and 6 asked "does it work for normal use?" A red-team asks "what is the worst it does when someone pushes?" AIHGle 2.0 recommends red-teaming for generative AI in healthcare; IMDA's Starter Kit describes how.

Plan before you attack

  • Scope: which product, version and features; what is out of scope.
  • Who might cause harm: a curious patient, a distressed patient, a carer asking for someone else, an outsider trying to extract data, a tampered document. These are your personas.
  • Categories of attack, so that coverage can be checked (table below).
  • Expected safe behaviour for every attack, written before you run it, so a pass or fail is not decided after the fact.
  • Rules of engagement: test environment only; invented data only; no real patient details in any prompt; findings shared only with the team; no working attacks published.
CategoryWhat the attacker triesMatching risk names

The four risks AIHGle 2.0 names for generative AI, hallucination, undesirable content, data disclosure and vulnerability to adversarial prompts, are the same four IMDA's Starter Kit treats as the baseline. The Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) Top 10 for LLM applications (2025) gives security names such as prompt injection (LLM01) and system prompt leakage (LLM07).

Record every attempt

A red-team log has one row per attack: an ID, category, persona, language, whether it takes one message or several, the attack, the expected safe behaviour, the result (pass, partial or fail), a severity from 1 (cosmetic) to 4 (could cause serious harm), and the fix and retest. Severity is about how bad the harm would be if it happened; likelihood is judged separately. Then:

  • Repeat important attacks. Outputs vary (Module 1), so one pass proves little. Module 8's "success on every repeat" applies.
  • Turn every failure into a test. Each confirmed failure becomes a permanent case in the release test set, so it cannot quietly return.
  • Look at coverage, not just count. Thirty attacks in English by one persona tell you less than fifteen spread across categories, languages and personas.

10.5 Fairness: who does the product serve worse?

Overall results hide groups. A model can be 90% accurate overall and much worse for one group (Module 5's "averages hide failures"). For a model that flags people, compare these rates for each group, using the four cells you met in Module 2:

Actually happened (for example, missed the appointment)Did not happen (attended) Flagged
TPTrue positive: correctly flagged
FPFalse positive: flagged but would have been fine
Not flagged
FNFalse negative: missed
TNTrue negative: correctly left alone
Selection rate
Share of the group flagged: (TP + FP) ÷ everyone in the group.
False positive rate (FPR)
Of the people for whom nothing would have happened, the share wrongly flagged: FP ÷ (FP + TN). Matters most when a flag is a burden: extra checks, a lower limit, an overbooked slot.
False negative rate (FNR) and recall
Of the people for whom it did happen, the share missed: FN ÷ (TP + FN). Recall is the share caught, 1 − FNR. Matters most when a flag brings help: a call, a priority review, support.
Precision
Of the people flagged, the share correctly flagged: TP ÷ (TP + FP).
Base rate
How often the event happens in the group, whatever the model says: (TP + FN) ÷ everyone in the group.

You cannot equalise everything. When groups have different base rates, a model generally cannot give them equal selection rates, equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates at the same time. Researchers proved this in 2016 and 2017 (Kleinberg and colleagues; Chouldechova). So fairness is a choice of which error matters most for this use, and it depends on what the flag triggers. The same model can be fair enough for a helpful phone call and unfair for a penalty.

A screening rule of thumb. Compare each group's rate with the best-served group. If a worse group's rate is less than four-fifths (0.8) of the best group's on a "good" measure (such as recall), or more than 1.25 times on a "bad" measure (such as false positive rate), investigate. This "four-fifths rule" comes from United States employment guidance; it is a trigger to look closer, not a legal test in Singapore and not a pass mark. Small groups give unstable rates; say how many cases each rate rests on.

Removing a sensitive attribute is not enough. Other inputs can stand in for it: postcode for income, preferred language for ethnicity, past missed appointments for shift work or caring duties. In a widely cited 2019 study in Science, Obermeyer and colleagues found that a United States algorithm used past healthcare spending to stand in for health need; because less was spent on Black patients with the same needs, the algorithm under-referred them for extra care. The fix was to change what the model predicted, not to delete a column.

What to do about a gap. In rough order of preference: fix the cause in the service (the model may be learning a real barrier you can remove); change what the flag triggers (help rather than penalty); change the inputs or what the model predicts; set group-aware thresholds only with legal and ethical advice; and in every case, monitor the gap after launch with a stop rule.

Worked example · Riverside's late-return predictor

Riverside built a model to flag members likely to return books late, so it could halve their borrowing limit. On 10,000 test loans, it compared members living more than 5 km from a branch with those living closer.

GroupLoansLate (base rate)Selection rateFalse positive rateRecall
Within 5 km8,00010%9.5%5.0%50%
More than 5 km2,00020%30.0%21.3%65%

Far-away members who would have returned on time were wrongly restricted four times as often. Distance makes returns harder; it is not a sign of carelessness. Riverside dropped the restriction and used the flag to send a renewal reminder and a map of drop boxes instead: a flag that helps rather than punishes. Late returns among far-away members fell, and the fairness gap stopped mattering as much because the flag no longer did harm.

10.6 The documents that carry the evidence

Governance documents are product documents. A PM owns them with clinical, legal and engineering colleagues, keeps them up to date with each release, and writes them for the reader who will act on them.

Your task. Give the AI Governance Committee the trust pack it asked for: no more than eight pages, plus your red-team log as an appendix. Use the five tools below; each one saves as you go and exports with your submission.

What your pack must contain

  1. Risk classification (about one page): for the medicine assistant and the missed-appointment predictor, each question in Part 10.3 answered with reasons, the controls each classification requires, your internal tier, and where your harm analysis sets a higher bar than the frameworks.
  2. Red-team (about 1.5 pages, plus the log as an appendix): a plan of 30 attacks on the assistant covering every category, with expected safe behaviour written first; at least 10 run against the practice stand-in with results; and a triage of engineering's eight findings with severity, fix, retest and whether each blocks expansion.
  3. Model card for the assistant (about two pages).
  4. Fairness check of the predictor (about 1.5 pages): groups compared, the measure that matters for each use and why, the gaps, what you think causes them, your decision on each use, and monitoring with a stop rule.
  5. One-page brief for the Ministry.

How it is marked

Each criterion is scored 0–4 using the programme rubric. A 0 or 1 on any criterion means a rewrite before grading.

CriterionFor a Distinction in this assignment
Decision qualityEach product is classified with reasons tied to its intended use, and the pack says what would change the classification. Clear decisions on expansion and on each use of the predictor, with conditions. The internal tier and controls go above the framework floor where the harm analysis says so.
EvidenceClassification cites the specific guideline tests. Red-team results come from attacks actually run, with expected behaviour written first. Fairness figures are calculated correctly by group, the measure matches what the flag triggers, and small groups are flagged. Model card results are broken down by language.
Risk handlingCoverage gaps in engineering's red-team are found and filled (languages, personas, multi-turn, content the assistant reads). Every finding has a severity, a fix and a retest, and the release rule is applied honestly. The pack addresses causes, not only scores: reminders patients cannot read, data used for a new purpose.
CommunicationThe committee can find each decision and its conditions in two minutes. The model card and brief are written for their readers, not for engineers. Eight pages plus the log; every acronym spelled out once.

        Step 4 · Close · about 60 minutes · Graded 40%Test and viva

        End-of-module test · 25%

        • Format: 10 Yes/No questions (1 mark each) and 5 short-answer questions (up to 3 marks each). Total 25 marks.
        • Rules: closed book, 30 minutes, taken once. A basic calculator is allowed. No notes, no AI tools.
        • Covers: everything in the lesson. Expect to classify a product you have not seen, say which frameworks are binding, calculate fairness rates for two groups and choose the measure that matters, plan red-team attacks by category, and say what a model card is missing.
        • Opens: once both checkpoint quizzes are at mastery. After emailing your assignment, email the course team to request the test paper and book your viva.

        Viva · 15%

        A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice") is a short spoken examination. Yours lasts 10 minutes, with an assessor or a trained peer.

        MinutesWhat happens
        0–2Without reading, you say whether the assistant may expand and on what conditions, and what you decided about each use of the predictor.
        2–7Questions on your pack. Expect "what would make the assistant a medical device?", "which of your attacks found something engineering missed?" and "why that fairness measure?"
        7–10The assessor changes one fact: a new incident, a new result or a new request. You say on the spot what it does to your decisions.

        It is marked on: whether you can defend your choices with evidence, whether you can explain the ideas in plain words, how you handle the changed fact, and how clearly you speak. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well.

        Going furtherReading and practice ideas

        Reading

        • Ministry of Health and Health Sciences Authority, Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare Guidelines 2.0 (March 2026). Read chapters 3, 4, 6.2 (the risk table) and 8.2 (generative AI). About 40 minutes.
        • AI Verify Foundation, resource library: the Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI, the Starter Kit for testing LLM-based applications, and a crosswalk between AI Verify and the NIST framework.
        • NIST, AI Risk Management Framework and the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1). Read the profile's list of 12 risks; about 20 minutes.
        • OWASP, Top 10 for LLM applications (2025). Read prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure and system prompt leakage; about 20 minutes.
        • Mitchell and colleagues, Model Cards for Model Reporting (2019). The original proposal; read sections 1 and 4. About 20 minutes.
        • Obermeyer and colleagues, Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations, Science (2019). How a proxy target created unfairness.
        • Future of Privacy Forum, The AI Act implementation timeline: what changes under the AI Omnibus? (2026). The new EU dates in one place.
        • HSA, guidance documents for medical devices, including GL-04 on software and machine learning.

        Practise on your own world

        • Classify three AI tools you use at work with the questions in Part 10.3, adapted to your sector. Which would a regulator in your industry care about most? Which has no named owner?
        • Write a model card for a tool you did not build. Use only what the vendor publishes. The gaps you cannot fill are your questions for the vendor.
        • Find the flag: think of a score your organisation uses to flag people (fraud, churn, credit, priority). What does the flag trigger, a help or a burden? Which error rate would you compare across groups?
