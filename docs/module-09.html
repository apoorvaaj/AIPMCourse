Modules 4 to 8 asked whether Harbourview's AI products work, and whether they are safe. This module asks whether they are worth what they cost. It is March. The consultation note drafter has run for ten weeks with six doctors. The medicine questions assistant has run at two clinics since October, and the board's plan to extend it was put on hold for the budget round. The Chief Financial Officer (CFO) has money for one rollout in the year from 1 April, and wants numbers that hold up whether AI prices fall or rise.

You will build a cost model for both initiatives, test it at three levels of adoption and three price levels, find the levers that protect the budget, and recommend which to fund first. Harbourview does not sell to patients, so the value side comes from staff time saved and calls avoided, not revenue. Step 1 · Lesson · about 90 minutesWhat AI really costs, and what it is worth

Part 1 explains why AI changes the economics of a product. Part 2 builds the cost of one task. Part 3 covers the levers that cut cost. Part 4 shows what happens as more people use a product and when prices move. Part 5 compares ways of pricing AI: per user, per use and per result. Part 6 builds a business case when nobody pays you for the product.

9.1 Why AI changes the economics of a product

Most software costs a lot to build and very little to run for one more user. Serving the thousandth person to open an app costs almost nothing extra. Products built on a large language model (LLM) are different. Every question sends text to a model, and the provider charges for it, measured in tokens, the word-pieces you met in Module 2. A product used twice as much costs roughly twice as much to run. Add the people who check the AI's work, answer the questions it hands over, and review samples for safety, and the cost of each extra task is real.

Reforge's essay on pricing AI products puts it this way: model costs raise the floor under any price, because each use has a cost. Its example is a monthly subscription with unlimited AI writing, where a single heavy user can cost the seller many times what they pay. The same logic applies inside an organisation. A free internal tool that staff love can blow its budget precisely because it succeeds.

Unit economics
The cost and value of one unit of the product: one question answered, one note drafted, one active user in a month. If one unit does not pay, more units will not fix it.
Cost to serve
Everything it costs to deliver one unit: model tokens, extra model calls, databases, hosting, and the staff time each unit creates.
Fixed cost
Costs that do not change with use in a month: hosting, a database subscription, a weekly safety review, a minimum licence fee.
Variable cost
Costs that rise with each unit: tokens, per-use fees, and the staff time for each handover or review.
One-off cost
Spending to get started: building, integrating, training staff, translating content. Also called set-up or capital cost.

Prices fall, bills often do not. Epoch AI, a research group that tracks AI trends, found that the price of reaching a fixed level of performance fell between 9 and 900 times a year between 2021 and early 2025, depending on the task. Yet many organisations' AI bills keep growing, because use grows faster, prompts get longer as teams add context, agents make many calls per task (Module 8), and teams move to newer, larger models. A product manager (PM) plans for prices going both ways.

9.2 Cost to serve: what one task really costs

List everything that happens when one task runs, and put a price on each part.

  1. 1The main model callvariable
    Input tokens (instructions, the user's data, retrieved passages, recent messages) plus output tokens (the answer). Output tokens usually cost four to five times as much as input tokens.
  2. 2Extra model callsvariable
    A router that sorts the request, an automated judge that checks a sample (Module 6), retries when a step fails, and agent steps (Module 8: a median of 7 tool calls).
  3. 3Systems around itmostly fixed
    The search database for retrieval, hosting, monitoring, logging.
  4. 4Peoplevariable and fixed
    Staff who answer handovers, doctors who correct drafts, reviewers who audit samples, the team that keeps content up to date. This is often the largest cost.
ModelSystemsPeople
model cost per task = (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price) ÷ 1,000,000
monthly cost = fixed costs + tasks per month × (model cost + extra calls + staff time per task)

Worked example · Ask Riverside (fictional, from Modules 2, 4 and 6)

Riverside Public Library's assistant sends about 2,000 input tokens and gets about 200 output tokens per question. Its model costs S$1 per million input tokens and S$5 per million output tokens. It answers 10,000 questions a month. Fixed costs are S$150 for the search database and S$400 for hosting and support.

Model cost per question: (2,000 × 1 + 200 × 5) ÷ 1,000,000 = S$0.003. Fifteen in every hundred questions go to the desk, and each takes a librarian 2 minutes at S$36 an hour (S$1.20 each): 0.15 × S$1.20 = S$0.18 per question.

Model S$30Fixed S$550Desk time for handovers S$1,800Total S$2,380 a month

The model is about 1% of the monthly cost. The staff time created by handovers is sixty times the model cost. When people say an AI feature "costs a fraction of a cent", ask what happens after the answer.

9.3 Levers that protect the budget

Each lever below reduces a cost line. Every one of them also changes what the product does, so each must pass your release gate from Module 6 before it goes live. A cheaper product that fails the gate is not cheaper; it moves the cost to whoever deals with the mistakes.

Worked example · Riverside's levers compared

Riverside caches its 600-token instructions (90% of requests reuse the cache), sends two passages instead of three (400 fewer tokens) and caps answers at 150 tokens instead of 200. Together these save about S$0.0011 a question: S$11 a month.

Separately, the team fixes the two most common reasons for needless handovers, and the handover rate falls from 15% to 10%. That saves 10,000 × 0.05 × S$1.20 = S$600 a month, more than fifty times the token levers. Rank levers by the cost line they attack, and attack the biggest line first.

9.4 Cost at different adoption levels, and when prices move

Adoption is the share of the people who could use a product who actually use it in a month. As adoption rises, fixed costs are shared among more users, so the cost per active user falls, and value usually rises with use. Two things break this smooth picture:

  • Step costs. A queue staffed by people has a capacity. Beyond it, you hire, pay for agency staff, or let replies slip. Cost jumps at that point.
  • Minimums and commitments. A licence with a minimum number of users, or a 12-month commitment, costs the same whether people use it or not.
break-even users = fixed monthly cost ÷ (value per active user − variable cost per active user)

Testing price changes. Model prices can fall (providers cut list prices) or rise (a model is retired and its successor costs more, a vendor passes costs on at renewal, exchange rates move). A simple test is to run the model with prices 50% lower and 50% higher. A business case is robust to price if the decision is the same in all three. It is fragile if it only works at one price.

Finding what swings the answer. Change one assumption at a time from a low to a high value and record the result. Draw the results as bars, longest at the top. This is a tornado chart. The longest bars are the assumptions worth measuring before you spend money; a bar that crosses zero is one that can flip the decision.

Worked example · What swings Riverside's case

Riverside has 20,000 members. An active member asks 3 questions a month. Each question costs S$0.183 (model plus desk time) and saves 0.5 desk enquiries of 4 minutes (S$1.20 of desk time). At 15% adoption, 3,000 active members, the net value is about S$8,600 a month. Each bar shows the net value when one assumption moves between the values in brackets.

Solid line: base case. Bars show the range of net value per month.

Halving or raising the model price by half moves the answer by about S$14. How many desk enquiries each question really saves moves it by more than S$8,000. Riverside should measure that before anything else.

9.5 Pricing models: per user, per use and per result

Whether you sell an AI product or buy one, the way it is priced decides who carries the risk when use, quality or costs change. Reforge describes four decisions in any monetisation design: how price scales with the value a customer gets (the value metric), what is included at each level, the amount, and when payment starts.

ModelYou pay forGood whenRisk for the buyerRisk for the seller
Per seatEach licensed user, each monthUse per person is steady and high; budgets must be predictablePaying for seats nobody uses; minimums and commitmentsHeavy users cost more to serve than they pay

OutcomeEach result: a resolved conversation, an accepted draftThe result is clear, measurable and agreedWho defines and measures the outcome, and what behaviour it rewardsPaying the cost of failed attempts
HybridA base fee plus usage or outcome chargesMost AI products todayTwo things to forecastComplexity to explain

An outcome price is only as good as its definition. Intercom, a customer-service software company, charges US$0.99 per conversation its AI agent, Fin, resolves. It counts a resolution when the customer confirms the answer helped, or when the customer leaves without asking for more help. The second kind is an assumption, not a fact, and a buyer should know how often it is used.

The buyer's crossover point. Divide the seat price by the price per unit. Above that many units per seat each month, the per-seat plan is cheaper; below it, pay per use. Then check minimums and commitments.

Worked example · Riverside buys a cataloguing assistant

A vendor offers AI that suggests subject tags for new books three ways: S$30 per librarian a month, S$0.05 per item, or S$0.08 per tag a librarian accepts (about 70% are accepted, so about S$0.056 per item). The crossover for seat versus per item is 30 ÷ 0.05 = 600 items per librarian a month. Riverside's 12 cataloguers handle about 400 items each, so per item costs S$20 per librarian against S$30 per seat. Per item wins today. The accepted-tag price looks fair, but it pays the vendor more when librarians accept more tags without checking; Module 7's automation bias, now with a price on it.

When you sell, price from the value to the customer, not from your cost. Cost sets the floor below which you lose money; value sets the ceiling. Check the margin for your heaviest users, not your average ones.

9.6 The business case when nobody pays

Harbourview, like many public bodies and internal teams, does not sell its AI products. The value is in what they save or make possible:

  • Staff time freed: minutes per task × tasks × the cost of that person's hour.
  • Demand avoided: calls, emails or visits that no longer happen.
  • Capacity gained: more appointments seen with the same staff.
  • Costs avoided: agency or overtime hours, errors, complaints.
  • Benefits to users that have no price: answers after hours, shorter waits, less burnout. Name them; do not invent a dollar value for them.

Not all freed time is money. Cash-releasing savings stop spending you would otherwise make: fewer agency hours, a vacancy not filled. Capacity-releasing savings free time for other work: the salary is still paid. Finance teams usually count capacity at a fraction of its cost, a realisation rate, unless you show exactly how the time is used. Three more rules: compare with what would have happened anyway, measured against a matched group if you can; do not count the same saving twice (a call avoided and the pharmacist's six minutes are one saving, not two); and subtract the new work the AI creates, such as handovers and audits.

payback months = one-off cost ÷ net value per month  ·  first-year cost = one-off cost + 12 × monthly running cost

Worked example · Riverside's case, counted honestly

The assistant avoids 5,000 desk enquiries of 4 minutes a month (333 hours) but creates 1,500 handovers of 2 minutes (50 hours): 283 hours freed. Of these, 60 hours replace casual Saturday staff Riverside would otherwise pay S$30 an hour, so S$1,800 is cash. The other 223 hours are capacity; at the council's 30% realisation rate and S$36 an hour they count as S$2,408. Value S$4,208 a month, running cost S$2,380 (part 9.2), net S$1,828 a month. With S$25,000 one-off, payback is about 14 months.

Counting all 283 hours at S$36 would have claimed S$10,188 a month, and a payback of under four months. The honest case is weaker but still positive, and it is the one finance will believe.

Fund in stages. A good recommendation releases money in tranches. Each tranche opens only when a measured figure is reached, and a stop rule closes it (Module 6's metrics tree). That way the business case is tested while it is being spent.

6. How the model calculatesShared rules for the page tools and the starter workbook

Use these rules so your numbers can be compared with others'. You may change any assumption in your own model if you say why, and show the shared-rule result alongside.

Medicine questions assistant

  • Active users = 120,000 × adoption. Questions = active users × 1.4.
  • Cost per question = (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price) ÷ 1,000,000, plus the judge: 20% × (1,600 × input price + 60 × output price) ÷ 1,000,000.
  • Running cost a month = questions × cost per question + S$4,360 fixed (S$2,500 + S$300 + S$1,560).
  • Pharmacist hours saved = (active users × 0.10 × 6 − questions × handover rate × 2.5) ÷ 60.
  • Value: the first 300 hours saved are cash at S$70 an hour (agency hours no longer bought). Hours beyond 300 are capacity: S$60 × 30%. If hours saved are negative, the extra hours cost S$70 each.
  • Handovers a day = questions × handover rate ÷ 30.

Consultation note drafter

  • Doctors using it = 260 × adoption, rounded up. Drafted notes = 95,000 × adoption × 85%.
  • Licence a month: per note = notes × S$0.38; per seat = the larger of 50 and doctors using it × S$40; per accepted note = notes × 58% × S$0.65.
  • Audit a month = notes × 2% × 5 ÷ 60 × S$150. Running cost = licence + audit + S$1,500.
  • Value = notes × minutes saved per note ÷ 60 × S$150 × 30%.

For both

  • The price test multiplies model token costs (assistant) or licence fees (note drafter) by 0.5 or 1.5.
  • Net value a month = value − running cost. First-year cost = one-off + 12 × running cost. Payback months = one-off ÷ net value a month (only if net is positive).
  • For simplicity, the adoption level applies from the first month. Say in your recommendation how a slower start would change the first year.

        Viva · 15%

        A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice") is a short spoken examination. Yours lasts 10 minutes, with an assessor or a trained peer.

        MinutesWhat happens
        0–2Without reading, you say which initiative to fund first, what it costs in the first year, and what would make you stop.
        2–7Questions on your model. Expect "which number in your model are you least sure of?", "why does the model price barely matter here?" and "how much of the time saved is cash?"
        7–10The assessor changes one fact: a price, a pilot result or a contract term. You say on the spot what it does to your recommendation.

        It is marked on: whether you can defend your choices with evidence, whether you can explain the ideas in plain words, how you handle the changed fact, and how clearly you speak. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well.

        Reading

        • Reforge, How to price your AI product (October 2025). The four monetisation levers, value metrics, and why AI raises the pricing floor. About 20 minutes.
        • Epoch AI, LLM inference price trends (March 2025). How fast prices fall for a fixed level of performance, and the caveats. About 10 minutes.

        Practise on your own world

        • Meeting notes for your team: work out the cost per meeting of an AI note-taker (an hour is about 9,000 words spoken), the time it saves, and how much of that is cash. Compare per seat and per minute pricing for your team's size.
        • An AI feature you would sell: pick a product you know and add one AI feature. Estimate the cost for an average and a heavy user. Where would you set the price floor? Would you charge per seat, per use or per result?
        • Your own AI bill: if you use an AI tool at work, find its usage report. Which 10% of users or tasks drive most of the cost? Which lever from Part 9.3 would you try first?
