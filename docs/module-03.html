Module 1 asked whether a problem needs AI. Module 2 asked how to build it. This module steps back to the question both of those assumed: is this the right problem, and do we have the data to solve it? Boards and executives often arrive with a solution already in mind ("build us a chatbot"). Your job is to find the problem underneath, test it cheaply, and check that the data can support an AI answer before anyone spends a build budget.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  1. Run discovery that tests the problem, not the AI solution, and turn what you learn into an opportunity solution tree.
  2. Assess whether data is ready for an AI product: whether it exists, whether you may use it, its quality, its labels, who it leaves out, and its privacy risks.
  3. Write a testable hypothesis with a stop condition, choose a cheap test, and make a go, pivot or stop decision.

Step 1 · Lesson · about 90 minutesFind the right problem, then check the data

Parts 1 to 4 cover discovery: separating problems from solutions, interviewing well, building an opportunity solution tree and testing assumptions cheaply. Part 5 covers data readiness. Part 6 shows how to turn all of it into a go, pivot or stop decision.

3.1 Problems, solutions and outcomes

Requests usually arrive as solutions: "an AI chatbot for bookings", "a model to read our feedback". A solution is one possible answer. Before you judge it, you need three things it leaves out.

Outcome
The measurable change the organisation wants, such as "fewer callers give up waiting" or "serious complaints are answered within three days". An outcome says nothing about how.
Opportunity
A need, pain point or wish of a real user, in their words: "I can't change an appointment the clinic chose for me." Opportunities are what discovery finds. A missing feature ("there is no chatbot") is not an opportunity.
Solution
Something you could build or change to address an opportunity. AI is one kind of solution. Better forms, clearer letters, a new rule or a staff process are others.

A useful test: if you cannot name the outcome and the opportunity a request serves, you are not yet ready to judge the solution. This is the core idea behind Teresa Torres's work on continuous discovery, which the reading for this module covers.

Why AI makes this harder. AI is exciting and well funded, so teams are tempted to start from "where can we use AI?" rather than "what problem matters most?". Leading AI product courses warn against this directly: validate the opportunity first, then ask whether AI is the best way to address it.

3.2 Discovery interviews that tell you the truth

People are poor at predicting what they will do, and kind enough to say they like your idea. Good discovery interviews avoid both traps by asking about real, recent events.

Weak question

Would you use a chatbot to book appointments?
Is it hard to book with us?
What features would you like?

Asks for a prediction or an opinion, suggests the answer, or hands design to the user.

Better question

Tell me about the last time you needed to see a doctor here. What did you do first?
What happened next? Where did you get stuck?
What did you do instead?

Asks for a specific story. You hear real behaviour, real words and workarounds.

Story-based interview
Ask about one specific past occasion, then keep asking "what happened next?". Workarounds (calling three times, borrowing a relative's login) are strong signals of a real problem.
Jobs to be done (JTBD)
A way of describing what someone is trying to get done in their life, independent of any product: "make sure my father's diabetes review happens without taking leave". It keeps you focused on the goal, not the tool.
Synthesis
Turning notes into findings. Write one observation per note, group similar notes (often called affinity mapping), name each group as an opportunity, and count how many people and records support it.

How strong is your evidence?

WeakerStronger
What people say they would doWhat people did last time, or what records show they did
One vivid interviewA pattern across several interviews and confirmed in data
Staff opinion about usersUsers' own words and behaviour
Only people who were easy to reachIncluding people who are hard to reach: older users, other languages, carers

Interviews and data work together. Interviews tell you why; records (call logs, booking data, complaints) tell you how often. Strong discovery uses both.

3.3 The opportunity solution tree

An opportunity solution tree (OST) is a one-page picture of discovery, described by Teresa Torres. It has four levels. Read it from the top down.

  1. Desired outcome at the top: one measurable outcome.
  2. Opportunities below it: the user needs and pain points that, if addressed, would move the outcome. Big opportunities can split into smaller ones.
  3. Solutions under each opportunity: several, so you compare rather than fall in love with one. At least one should be a non-AI option.
  4. Assumption tests under each solution: the quickest way to find out whether it would work.

Worked example · Riverside Public Library (fictional)

You met Riverside in Module 2. Its board now asks for "an AI chatbot so members can ask if their reserved book is ready". Twelve member interviews and a year of reservation records tell a different story: 28% of reserved books are never collected, mostly because members miss the email saying the book is waiting, or cannot get to the branch within the seven-day collection window.

  • OutcomeReserved books collected rises from 72% to 85% within six months
    • Opportunity"I don't notice when my book is ready"
      • SolutionText message alert, sent by SMS (Short Message Service), as well as emailnon-AI
        • TestSend texts for half of reservations for four weeks; compare collection rates
      • SolutionChatbot answering "is my book ready?"AI
        • TestCount how often members ask this today: only 40 enquiries a month
    • Opportunity"I can't get there within seven days"
      • SolutionLocker collection open until 10 pmnon-AI
      • SolutionPredict who will miss the window and extend it for themAI
        • TestFirst try a simple rule: extend for anyone who missed one before

The board's chatbot sits on the tree, but discovery shows it addresses a small opportunity. A text alert, which needs no AI, is tested first. The prediction model from Module 2 survives as a later option, to be tried only if the simple rule leaves a gap.

Common mistakes on a tree

  • An outcome that is really a solution ("launch a chatbot") or cannot be measured ("better experience").
  • Opportunities written as missing features ("no AI assistant") instead of user needs.
  • Only one solution per opportunity, so nothing is compared.
  • Opportunities with no evidence behind them. Each should trace back to interviews or data.

3.4 Assumptions, hypotheses and cheap tests

Every solution rests on assumptions. Before building, list them and test the riskiest ones first. Five kinds of assumption are worth checking for any AI product.

KindThe questionExample for a booking chatbot
DesirabilityWill people want it and use it?Older patients will message a bot rather than phone
UsabilityCan people use it without help?Patients can describe what they need in their own words
FeasibilityCan we build it, including the data?The booking system lets software change clinic-booked appointments
ViabilityIs it worth it for the organisation?Enough calls move to the bot to free staff time
Ethics and safetyCould it harm anyone?The bot never delays someone describing an emergency

Cheap ways to test an AI idea before building it

Wizard of Oz test
Users interact with what looks like the AI product, but a person secretly writes the replies. You learn what people ask, in what words, and whether they finish the task, before building anything. The optional lab on this page is a Wizard of Oz test.
Concierge test
Like Wizard of Oz, but users know a person is helping. Useful for learning the job in detail.
Fake door test
Add a button or link for a feature that does not exist yet ("Change this appointment") and count clicks, then explain it is coming. Measures demand honestly if you tell people afterwards.
Data spike
Pull a small sample of real records (for example, 200 comments) and look at them by hand. Fast, and often decisive for data readiness.
A/B test
Show version A to some users and version B to others, then compare. Best once you have something real to compare.

Writing a testable hypothesis

A hypothesis turns an assumption into something a test can prove wrong. Use this pattern:

We believe [users] have [problem] because [evidence]. If we [solution or test], then [measurable change] within [time]. We will stop or change course if [stop condition].

Worked example · Riverside text alerts

We believe members miss reserved books because the "ready" email goes unread (9 of 12 interviewees said so; email open rate is 31%). If we also send a text message for half of all reservations for four weeks, collection for that half will rise by at least 6 percentage points. We will stop if the rise is under 3 points, or if more than 2% of members ask to stop the texts.

Notice the stop condition is written before the test starts. If you decide what counts as failure after seeing the results, almost any result can be called a success.

3.5 Data readiness: six questions before you build

An AI product is only as good as the data it learns from, is tested on, or reads from. "We have lots of data" is not the same as "we have data ready for this job". Check six dimensions.

DimensionQuestions to askTypical warning sign
1. Availability and accessDoes the data exist? Enough of it? Can we get it out, and how fresh is it?"It's in the vendor's system; we've never exported it."
2. Rights and permissionWas it collected for a purpose that covers this use? Do contracts and consent allow it? What does the data protection officer (DPO) say?Data collected for billing being reused for marketing predictions
3. QualityIs it complete, accurate, consistent and up to date?Fields that changed meaning when a system was replaced
4. Labels (ground truth)Do we know the right answer for enough examples to train or test? Who decides, and do they agree?Two experts label the same items and disagree a third of the time
5. Coverage and fairnessDoes it represent everyone the product will serve: ages, languages, sites, channels? Are the rare but important cases there?A survey that older patients rarely answer
6. Privacy and securityDoes it contain personal data we do not need? Can we remove it? Where will it be processed and for how long?Free text containing identity numbers and diagnoses

Scoring each dimension

ScoreMeaning
3Ready. No significant gaps.
2Usable with known gaps. Fix alongside building.
1Major gaps. Fix before building; weeks to months of work.
0Blocker. Missing or not allowed, and no fix in sight.

A single 0 stops the project however high the other scores are. You cannot build on data you are not allowed to use, however clean it is.

Three ideas about labels that catch teams out

  1. If people cannot agree, the model cannot be judged. Ask two trained people to label the same sample. The share of items they label the same way is called agreement between labellers (inter-rater agreement). If it is low, fix the categories and instructions (the codebook) before you automate anything.
  2. Rare cases need deliberate searching. If 1 in 100 records is the case you care about, a random sample of 500 contains about 5. To test a model on 100 examples you would need to read about 10,000 records at random. Instead, look for likely examples on purpose, for instance with keyword searches or from cases already escalated.
  3. Labelling has a real cost. Estimate it before you promise a date.
labelling cost = items × (1 + share double-labelled) × minutes per item ÷ 60 × hourly cost

Worked example · Riverside reservation records

Before building the prediction model from Module 2, the library runs a readiness check on three years of reservation records.

DimensionScoreEvidence and fix

Total 13 of 18, no blocker: ready with conditions. If labels had needed staff to read records, the cost would be, for 2,000 items at 1 minute each with 20% double-labelled and staff costing S$36 (Singapore dollars) an hour:

2,000 × 1.2 × 1 ÷ 60 × 36 = 40 hours × S$36 = S$1,440

Personal data in Singapore. Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) limits how organisations collect, use and share personal data. Using existing data to build or test AI raises questions about purpose, consent and the exceptions the Act allows. The Personal Data Protection Commission (PDPC) published advisory guidelines in 2024 on using personal data in AI recommendation and decision systems, listed in the reading. As a product manager you do not decide the legal answer; you make sure the data protection officer is asked early, with a clear description of the data and the use. Module 10 covers governance in depth. Nothing on this page is legal advice.

3.6 The decision: go, pivot or stop

Discovery ends in a decision, not a slide of findings.

DecisionWhenWhat you say next
GoA real, frequent opportunity; the solution's riskiest assumptions survived a test; data is ready or the gaps are fixableWhat you will build first, what it must prove and by when
PivotThe outcome still matters, but the evidence points to a different opportunity, user group, solution or data sourceWhat changes, what stays, and the next test
StopThe opportunity is small, an assumption failed, or a data blocker cannot be fixedWhy, what was learned, and what would reopen it

A pivot is not a failure. Keeping the outcome while changing the route to it is the most common, and often the most valuable, result of good discovery. "Go with conditions" is also a fair answer, as long as the conditions are specific and someone owns them.

Viva · 15%

A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice") is a short spoken examination. Yours lasts 10 minutes, with an assessor or a trained peer.

MinutesWhat happens
0–2You explain both decisions in your own words, without reading.
2–7Questions on your choices. Expect "why not just build what the board asked for?", "what is your evidence for that opportunity?" and "how will you find enough serious complaints to test on?"
7–10A new fact you have not seen, such as a test result, a delay or a new instruction. You say whether it changes either decision, and why.

It is marked on: whether you can defend your choices with evidence, whether you can explain the ideas in plain words, how you handle the new fact, and how clearly you speak. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well.

Optional lab · 1–2 hours · not assessedRun a Wizard of Oz test

Play Harbourview's booking assistant yourself, by chat, with two volunteers who think they might be talking to an automated assistant or know you are role-playing one. You will learn what people actually type, where they get stuck, and how much of the job needs AI at all.

Keep it fictional. Volunteers play the patients on the cards below. Ask them not to share real health details. If you tell volunteers afterwards that a person was replying, say so plainly; the test is about learning, not deceiving.

  1. Open a chat with each volunteer on any messaging app. Send: "Hi, I'm the Harbourview booking assistant. How can I help?"
  2. Give the volunteer one scenario card at a time, without showing them the others.
  3. Reply only as the assistant, following the rulebook. Keep replies short. Record what happens.
Your rulebook as the assistantFollow it strictly
  • You can: book, change or cancel appointments; explain what each service is in plain words; give opening hours.
  • You cannot: give medical advice, or say whether a symptom is serious.
  • If anyone mentions chest pain, difficulty breathing, severe bleeding or confusion, reply only: "This could be an emergency. Please call 995 now."
  • For any other symptom, reply: "I can't advise on symptoms. I can connect you to a nurse, or book you an appointment."
  • If you are unsure what they want, ask one short question. Never guess.
Four scenario cards for volunteersGive one at a time

Card 1. You are 70. You got a letter saying your diabetes review is next Tuesday at 9 am, but you have a family event. You want a later day.

Card 2. You look after your mother. You want to book her flu vaccination at the clinic near her flat, on a Saturday.

Card 3. You have had a cough for a week and feel a bit breathless when climbing stairs. You want to know if you should come in today.

Card 4. You want "the blood test for cholesterol" but don't know what the clinic calls it.

Going furtherReading and practice ideas

Reading

  • Teresa Torres, Continuous Discovery Habits (2021), chapters 4–6 on outcomes, interviewing and mapping the opportunity space. For a short version, read her opportunity solution tree overview on Product Talk. About 15 minutes.
  • Google People + AI Research (PAIR), People + AI Guidebook, the chapters on user needs and on data collection and evaluation. About 40 minutes.
  • Timnit Gebru and colleagues, Datasheets for Datasets. A widely used set of questions to ask about any dataset. Skim the questions in section 3. About 20 minutes.
  • Personal Data Protection Commission, Advisory Guidelines on use of Personal Data in AI Recommendation and Decision Systems (2024). Read the parts on developing and testing AI systems. About 30 minutes.

Practise on your own world

Try an outcome, a three-opportunity tree and a six-dimension readiness check on one of these product ideas, or on a request from your own workplace, and bring it to your viva if you like.

  • "AI to answer staff information technology (IT) tickets": before building, which ticket types are most common, and how many are password resets a self-service form would fix?
  • Contract clause finder for a legal team: are past contracts labelled? Who would label them, and would two lawyers agree?
  • Trade document checker for a bank: checks shipping and invoice documents against a letter of credit. How rare are real discrepancies, and how would you find enough examples to test on?
