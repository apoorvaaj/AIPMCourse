In Module 4 you wrote the requirements for Harbourview's medicine questions assistant and linked each one to a test. The assistant has now been live at two clinics for two weeks. This module is about the question every artificial intelligence (AI) product team faces at this point: is it working, and if not, what exactly is wrong? The answer does not start with a dashboard. It starts with reading what the product actually said to real people, one conversation at a time.

The method you will learn is called error analysis. It comes from social science research and from machine learning practice, and it is the core of the evaluation courses taught by Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar and by Reforge, which this module draws on.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  1. Review real outputs and label each one pass or fail with a specific reason, before reaching for generic scores.
  2. Group failures into a failure taxonomy, count how often each type happens, and rank the types by frequency and severity to choose what to fix first.
  3. Build a starting test set from requirements and structured dimensions, including before a product has any users, and keep it honest as the product changes.

Step 1 · Lesson · about 90 minutesLook at the data, then decide what to measure

Part 1 explains why evaluation starts from real outputs. Part 2 covers what to collect and how to sample it. Parts 3 and 4 teach the two steps of error analysis: writing notes on each output, then grouping the notes into failure types. Part 5 covers choosing what to fix. Part 6 turns everything into test sets you can rerun.

5.1 Why start from data, not metrics

It is tempting to evaluate an AI product with a ready-made score: a "helpfulness" rating, a "faithfulness" percentage, a similarity number from a vendor dashboard. These scores are easy to produce and hard to act on. A helpfulness score of 4.3 out of 5 does not tell you whether the assistant ever missed an emergency, or which kind of question it gets wrong, or what to change on Monday.

The alternative is to begin by reading. Practitioners describe evaluation as a loop with three moves:

  1. 1Analysepeople read outputs
    Review a sample of real outputs. Note what goes wrong. Group the notes into failure types and count them.
  2. 2Measuretests and checks
    Build tests for the failure types that matter: code checks, test sets, and later automated judges (Module 6).
  3. 3Improvechange the product
    Change rules, sources, instructions or design. Rerun the tests. Then go back to step 1 with fresh outputs, because fixes create new failures.
PeopleFixed checksProduct changes

The order matters. If you choose metrics first, you measure what was easy to imagine, not what is actually going wrong. If you analyse first, the failures you find tell you which metrics are worth building. That is why the first evaluation step for a new AI product is usually a person, often the product manager (PM) and a domain expert, reading a hundred or so outputs.

Three reasons AI products go wrong. Shreya Shankar and Hamel Husain describe three gaps. A gap in understanding: the team has not looked closely enough at its inputs and outputs to know what is happening. A gap in specification: the instructions, rules or sources never said what the team meant, so the product could not have known. A gap in generalisation: the instructions were clear, but the model still fails on some inputs. Error analysis closes the first gap and tells you which of the other two you are facing, which matters because they have different fixes.

5.2 Traces: what to collect and how to sample

A trace is the full record of one interaction: everything you need to understand why the product did what it did. For a system like Harbourview's, which searches approved leaflets and passes passages to a large language model (LLM), a design called retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), a useful trace contains:

Part of the traceWhy you need it
Who asked, in what context (language, medicine list, clinic, day)Many failures only happen for some users or situations
The question, exactly as typedTypos, slang and mixed languages are part of the input
What was retrieved: passages, their source, their status (for example, review overdue)Separates "found the wrong thing" from "found the right thing and misused it"
Which rules fired (emergency, handover, scope checks)Shows whether a fixed rule or the model made the decision
The answer shown, with flags (handed over, emergency message) and response timeThe thing you are judging
Version of the instructions, sources and modelLets you link failures to changes

How to choose which traces to read

  • Random sample. The only way to estimate how often each failure happens across all users. Start here.
  • Targeted sample. Traces with a thumbs-down, a handover, an unusually long answer, a slow response, or a low search score. Good for finding failures quickly, but the rates you count are not representative, so report them separately.
  • Gap sample. Traces from groups the random sample barely covers, such as rarely used languages.

How many?

A common starting point is about 100 traces. Keep reading until new traces stop revealing new kinds of failure. Researchers call this point saturation. If trace 90 shows a failure you have never seen, you are not done. If the last 20 traces only repeat failure types you already have, you probably are, for this round.

Privacy is part of the method. Traces from a health product contain personal health information. Agree in advance who may read them, where, and how long they are kept. In Module 4 Harbourview's data protection officer (DPO) limited log access to the pharmacy safety team for 12 months. Your error analysis happens inside those limits.

5.3 Open coding: pass or fail, with a reason

The first step of error analysis is borrowed from qualitative research and called open coding. For each trace, you write down whether it passes or fails and, if it fails, a short note on what went wrong. You do not use a predefined list of categories yet. You write what you see.

Four rules that make the notes useful

  1. Judge pass or fail, not a score. A 1-to-5 scale invites arguments about whether something is a 3 or a 4, and the average hides the failures you care about. "Would we be happy for this answer to reach a patient?" is a question people can answer consistently.
  2. Note the first thing that went wrong. If the search found the wrong leaflet and the answer then gave the wrong dose, the first failure is the search. Fixing it often fixes what follows. Mention later problems too, but label the first.
  3. Be specific. "Bad answer" is useless when you reach Part 4. "Told patient to stop metformin before scan; not in passage" can be grouped, counted and fixed.
  4. Judge against the requirements. The yardstick is the requirements document, not your taste. If an answer feels wrong but breaks no requirement, note that too: you may have found a missing requirement.

Who labels matters. One person with real domain knowledge, such as a pharmacist, should own the final call on what counts as a failure. Husain and Shankar call this person a "benevolent dictator". A committee produces vague, averaged judgements. A second reviewer is still useful: they label a sample independently, and the two compare disagreements, which often reveal unclear requirements.

5.4 Axial coding: from notes to a failure taxonomy

Once you have notes for every failing trace, the second step, axial coding, groups them into a small number of failure types. The result is a failure taxonomy: a named list of the ways your product fails, each with a definition, a count and examples.

  1. Read all the failure notes together and put similar ones side by side. Riverside's traces 2 and 6 belong together; 4 and 7 do not.
  2. Name each group by what went wrong, not by topic. "Claims actions it cannot take" is a failure type. "Renewals" is a topic, and topic groups mix passes and fails.
  3. Write a one-line definition with what is in and what is out, so that someone else would sort the same traces the same way.
  4. Count. Assign every failing trace to one type (its first failure). Note secondary problems separately if they matter.
  5. Revise. Merge types that are really the same; split a type that hides two different causes. Five to ten types is usual for a first round.

A count of zero is worth recording when a requirement is a MUST NEVER: it tells you the sample did not find it, not that it cannot happen. Module 4's rule of three applies: zero in 100 random traces only says the true rate is probably below about 3%.

Can an AI tool help? Yes, for the grouping step. You can paste your notes into an AI assistant and ask it to suggest groups. Treat the suggestion as a first draft: check every assignment, rename vague groups, and never let a tool write the open-coding notes for you. The value of open coding comes from a person reading the traces.

5.5 Choosing what to fix

A taxonomy gives you counts. Counts alone would tell you to fix the most common failure first, which is often the wrong call. Two more things decide the order: how much harm each failure can do, and what kind of fix it needs.

Severity

Agree a severity scale with the domain owner before you rank. Harbourview's pharmacy uses four levels:

LevelMeaningExample
4 · CriticalCould cause serious harm or delay emergency careA possible emergency does not get the emergency message
3 · HighCould lead a patient to take a medicine unsafely or skip needed professional adviceWrong dose instruction; a pregnancy question not handed over
2 · MediumPatient may misunderstand or be left out; harm unlikelyAnswer in the wrong language
1 · LowFriction, cost or loss of trustAnswer too long; a needless handover

A simple ranking rule: treat any critical failure as stop-the-line, fixed before anything else whatever its count. Rank the rest by count × severity, then sense-check the order. Do not forget that low-severity failures can carry big operational costs: a needless handover costs a pharmacist's time, and Module 4 showed that pharmacist time is the tightest limit on rollout.

What kind of fix?

Failure looks likeProbable gapTypical fix
The rules or instructions never covered this caseSpecificationChange a fixed rule, instruction, source or policy. Often cheap and quick.
A fixed rule fired wrongly or not at all (for example a keyword list)Specification (in the rule)Fix the rule, then test it on many phrasings
The wrong passage was retrievedSearch designFilter by the exact product, improve how leaflets are split and labelled
Instructions were clear, the right passage was found, and the model still got it wrongGeneralisationOutput checks, better examples, possibly a stronger model; build an automated judge (Module 6)

This table also protects you from a common trap: "let's switch to a bigger model". A bigger model will not fix a keyword list that is missing a word, or a search that returns the wrong leaflet.

How sure can you be about a count?

If 7 of 100 traces show a failure, the true rate across all conversations is not exactly 7%. With a sample this size it could reasonably be anywhere from about 3% to 14%. A statistical tool called the Wilson interval gives this likely range. The taxonomy tool on this page calculates it for you. Two practical lessons: small counts have wide ranges, so do not over-interpret the difference between 4 and 6; and if a decision depends on the exact rate, read more traces.

5.6 Test sets: from findings to tests you can rerun

Error analysis tells you what is going wrong today. A test set lets you check, every time the product changes, that it has not gone wrong again. A test case is an input plus the expected behaviour, not an exact expected wording, because an LLM will phrase a good answer differently each time.

Golden dataset
A set of test cases whose expected behaviour has been agreed by the domain owner. Also called a reference set.
Regression set
Cases that already pass and must keep passing. Catches fixes that break something else.
Targeted set
Many variations of one failure type, for example 60 food-and-drink questions, to check a fix properly.
Safety set
Deliberately difficult cases for a MUST NEVER, sized using Module 4's rule of three.
Held-out set
Cases the team does not look at while making changes, kept for the final release check.

Building a test set before you have users

Before launch there are no real traces. You can still build a strong starting set by combining the requirements (Module 4's examples are a seed), domain experts' knowledge of what people really ask, and synthetic questions: questions written, by people or with AI help, to cover situations you expect. The trick is to be systematic rather than writing whatever comes to mind.

  1. Choose three or four dimensions that change how the product should behave. For Harbourview: patient type, question type, and scenario (straightforward, ambiguous, out of scope, emergency, attempt to misuse).
  2. List values for each, deliberately including the unusual ones.
  3. Combine them into tuples: one value from each dimension. "Aged 80+ on six medicines × missed dose × ambiguous wording."
  4. Write a question for each tuple in the user's own voice, with the expected behaviour and the requirement it tests. Write the first 10 to 20 by hand before asking an AI tool to help with more, so you know what good looks like.
  5. Review every question. Remove duplicates, unrealistic ones, and ones that are all too easy. Spend more cases on risky combinations.

Keeping test sets honest

  • Never put test questions into the model's instructions as examples. The product will pass the test and fail the real thing.
  • Keep a held-out set for release decisions, and refresh it from time to time.
  • Replace synthetic cases with real ones as traces arrive. Real users ask things nobody imagined.
  • Version the set and record who agreed each expected behaviour.
  • Check coverage: count cases per dimension value. An empty cell is a group you know nothing about.

Viva · 15%

A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice") is a short spoken examination. Yours lasts 10 minutes, with an assessor or a trained peer.

See what an AI tool does well and badly when you ask it to write test questions, using the cold-start brief for the consultation note drafter (case section 5) or your own Tool 3 dimensions for the medicine assistant.

Keep it fictional. Do not enter any real patient information, real consultation recordings or anything about real people. Use only the brief on this page.

  1. Write your three dimensions and their values. Write five test cases yourself first, so you know what good looks like.
  2. Open any public AI assistant and paste the prompt below, filling in your dimensions.
  3. Review all 20 results. Mark each one keep, fix or drop, and note why. Count duplicates, unrealistic cases and cases that are too easy.
Prompt to pasteFill in the square brackets

I am building a test set for [product, for example: an AI tool that drafts a doctor's visit note from a recorded consultation at a Singapore polyclinic]. The dimensions are: (1) [dimension 1]: [values]; (2) [dimension 2]: [values]; (3) [dimension 3]: [values]. First, list 20 different combinations, using at least one unusual value from each dimension and spending more on risky combinations. Then, for each combination, write one realistic test case in the user's own words or situation, and the behaviour we should expect. Do not repeat the same wording. Practise on your own world

Take 30 outputs from an AI tool you use at work or at home (summaries, drafted emails, search answers). Label each pass or fail with a note, group the failures, and write five test cases for the top failure type. Bring it to your viva if you like.

  • Payment screening alerts summariser for a bank: an LLM writes a one-paragraph summary of each sanctions-screening alert for the analyst. What would its failure types be, and which would be critical?
  • Meeting notes assistant: label 30 summaries of meetings you attended. How often does it record a decision that was not made?
  • Customer email triage: design a test set from three dimensions (customer type, request type, tone) before the tool goes live.
