Specifying AI products: the AI requirements document

A feature list tells engineers what to build. An AI product also needs a description of how it must behave, what it must never do, and how anyone will know it is doing so.

Term I asked whether a problem needs artificial intelligence (AI), how to build it, and whether it is the right problem with the right data. Term II starts where building starts: writing down exactly what the product must do. For ordinary software, a product requirements document (PRD) lists features and the screens that deliver them. For an AI product that is not enough, because the same question can get a different answer each time. You have to specify behaviour, set limits on it, and say how each limit will be tested.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  1. Write requirements that specify behaviour, not just features, using clear rule strengths (must, must never, should prefer) and worked examples.
  2. Turn safety and policy rules into guardrails, with deliberate designs for refusing, handing over to a person, and falling back when part of the system fails.
  3. Write acceptance criteria that a test can check, including limits on cost and response time, and link every requirement to its test.

Step 1 · Lesson · about 90 minutesSpecify the behaviour, then prove it

Parts 1 and 2 explain why AI products need a different kind of requirements document and how to write behaviour rules. Part 3 covers guardrails, refusals, handovers and fallbacks. Part 4 turns rules into acceptance criteria a test can check. Part 5 covers cost, speed and other operating limits. Part 6 ties every requirement to its test.

4.1 Why a feature list is not enough

A traditional requirement such as "the user can reset their password" is either met or not. Test it once and you know. An AI requirement such as "the assistant answers questions about the patient's medicines" can be met beautifully on one question and dangerously on the next, because a large language model (LLM) produces its answer fresh each time. So an AI requirements document has to do three extra jobs.

A traditional PRD saysAn AI PRD must also say
What features exist and what each screen doesHow the product behaves across the range of things people will ask, including the odd, hostile and dangerous ones
What happens when the user clicksWhat it must never do, what it does when it should not answer, and who it hands over to
"Done" means the feature works in testingHow good is good enough, measured on a named set of test cases, with a pass rate, a cost limit and a speed limit

Leading templates converge on the same sections. Product School's AI PRD template, Miqdad Jaffer's widely shared AI PRD template and Marily Nika's AI Product Academy template all add sections for model behaviour, data, evaluation, guardrails and cost to the usual problem, users and scope. The assignment uses a structure drawn from all three.

Behaviour specification
The part of the document that says how the product must act in each kind of situation, written as rules with examples.
Acceptance criterion
The measurable condition that shows a requirement is met, for example "on 200 test questions, at least 95% pass".
Non-functional requirement
A limit on how the product operates rather than what it does: cost, response time, where data is processed, how long logs are kept.

The prompt is not the specification. The instructions given to the model (often called the system prompt) are one way of implementing the requirements, alongside rules, search settings and checks. If the requirements live only inside the prompt, nobody outside engineering can review them, and nobody can test whether a change to the prompt broke one. Write the requirements first, in plain language; the prompt follows from them.

4.2 Behaviour rules: must, must never, should prefer

Every behaviour rule needs a clear strength, so that engineers and testers know which ones can bend. These words come from a convention for internet standards, Request for Comments 2119 (RFC 2119), simplified here into four levels.

StrengthMeaningTypical pass threshold
MUSTRequired behaviour. Failing it is a defect.A high rate, such as 95–99% of relevant test cases
MUST NEVERForbidden behaviour. One failure can cause harm.Zero failures on a large, deliberately difficult test set, plus a safeguard that does not depend on the model alone
SHOULD PREFERThe default, which may give way when there is a good reason. Name the reason.A target rate or average, such as "median answer under 120 words"
MAYAllowed but not required.Usually not tested

The shape of a good behaviour rule

[ID] [STRENGTH] · When [situation], the product [behaviour]. Acceptance: on [test set], [measure] [threshold], checked by [method].

You met Riverside in Modules 2 and 3. It now plans a chat assistant that answers members' questions about library services, using the library's own rules pages. Three of its requirements:

VagueWhy it failsTestable
"Answers should be accurate."Accurate against what? How often?MUST: every factual statement is supported by the cited page. At least 95% of 200 test questions fully supported, judged by a librarian.

Rules are abstract; examples show what you mean. Every AI PRD should include a set of example inputs with the expected behaviour, covering typical cases, edge cases and cases the product must refuse. These examples later seed the test set you will build in Module 5.

4.3 Guardrails: refusing, handing over and falling back

A guardrail is any control that keeps the product inside its allowed behaviour. Good guardrails are layered, so that one failure is caught by the next. In Module 2 you walked a system diagram for exactly this reason.

  1. 1Before the model: input rulesrule
    Keyword and pattern checks for emergencies, scope checks (is this medicine on the patient's list?), and blocks on obvious attempts to override instructions (prompt injection).
  2. 2What the model is givenrule
    Only approved sources, only the signed-in person's data.
  3. 3Model instructionsAI
    The behaviour rules written into the model's instructions. Useful, but the model can still break them.
  4. 4After the model: output checksrule or AI
    Does every dose in the answer appear in the cited passage? Is there a cited source at all? Is the answer too long?
  5. 5Peopleperson
    Handover to a professional, and regular review of a sample of real conversations.
Fixed ruleAI stepPerson

A MUST NEVER rule should never rely on layer 3 alone. If a rule matters enough to forbid, put a check in layer 1 or 4 as well.

Three different things, often confused

Refusal
The product declines to do something it should not do, such as advise stopping a medicine. A good refusal says what it cannot do, why in a few words, and what the person can do instead.
Handover (handoff)
The product passes the conversation to a person, such as a pharmacist, with the question attached so the person does not have to start again. Specify who receives it, when they reply, and what the user is told meanwhile. A handover goes to people with limited time, so its volume is a requirement too.
Fallback
What happens when part of the system fails or cannot produce a safe answer: the search step times out, no source is found, the model is unavailable. "Something went wrong" is not a fallback design.

A response ladder

Specify which rung each kind of question lands on. Most products need all five.

  1. 1Answer fullyIn scope, source found, low risk. Answer and cite the source.
  2. 2Answer with a pointerAnswer, plus "ask a pharmacist if…" for a named situation.
  3. 3Partial answer and hand overGive what the source safely supports, then pass the rest to a person.
  4. 4Decline and hand overOut of scope or too risky to answer at all. Say so plainly and hand over.
  5. 5Emergency stopPossible emergency. Show only the emergency message and number. No other content to read first.

Worked example · writing the words people see

Requirements should include the exact wording of fixed messages, because wording changes behaviour. Compare two refusals for Riverside's fine-waiver case:

Weak

I'm sorry, I cannot help with that request.

No reason, no next step. Members try again with different words, or give up.

Specified

Fine waivers are decided by our librarians. I've sent your message to them with your account details, and they'll reply by email within one working day.

Says who decides, what happens next and when.

4.4 Acceptance criteria a test can check

An acceptance criterion turns a rule into a pass or fail. A useful pattern, borrowed from software testing, is Given–When–Then:

Given a signed-in member with two items on loan
When they ask "what do I have out at the moment?"
Then the answer lists exactly those two items with due dates, and no others.

For AI products, a single Given–When–Then is one test case. The acceptance criterion is a rate across many cases: "at least 97% of 150 loan questions list exactly the member's items".

Three ways to check a test case

MethodWhat it isGood forLimits
Code checkA simple program checks the output: is a source named, is the answer under 150 words, does a dose in the answer appear in the source?Format, length, presence of required text, handover flags. Fast, cheap, exact.Cannot judge meaning or tone
Model-based judgeA second AI model marks the answer against a written rule, for example "does this answer advise stopping a medicine?"Meaning-based rules at scale. Module 6 covers how to build and check one.Must itself be checked against human judgements
Human reviewA qualified person marks each answer.High-stakes rules, building the first test sets, checking the judgeSlow and costly

How many tests prove a "must never"?

Suppose you run 300 test questions and see no harmful answers. Can you say the product never gives harmful answers? No. You can only say the true rate is probably low. A handy approximation called the rule of three says: if you see zero failures in N independent tests, you can be 95% confident the true failure rate is below about 3 ÷ N.

zero failures in N tests → true rate below about 3 ÷ N (95% confidence)
Tests with zero failuresYou can claim the rate is below aboutAt 18,000 answers a month, that still allows up to

Two consequences for your requirements. First, a MUST NEVER needs a test set sized for the claim you want to make, and it should be deliberately difficult, full of the phrasings most likely to cause the failure. Second, because no test set can prove zero, a MUST NEVER also needs a safeguard outside the model and live monitoring after launch. The test-size calculator in the assignment does this arithmetic for you.

4.5 Operating limits: cost, speed and the rest

AI products have running costs for every question, and response times that vary. Both belong in the requirements, because they shape design choices and trade off against quality.

Token
A chunk of text, roughly three-quarters of an English word, which model vendors use to charge. Input tokens are what you send (instructions, sources, the question); output tokens are the answer.
Cost per question
Input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price. Add fixed monthly costs, such as the database used to search sources, to get a monthly total.
Median response time
The time within which half of answers arrive.
95th percentile (p95) response time
The time within which 95% of answers arrive. It describes the slow answers people remember, so it is usually the better limit to specify.

Worked example · Ask Riverside's budget

Each question sends about 2,000 input tokens and gets about 200 output tokens back. The model costs S$1 (Singapore dollars) per million input tokens and S$5 per million output tokens. The library expects 10,000 questions a month and pays S$150 a month for its search database.

per question = 2,000 × 1 ÷ 1,000,000 + 200 × 5 ÷ 1,000,000 = S$0.002 + S$0.001 = S$0.003
per month = 10,000 × S$0.003 + S$150 = S$30 + S$150 = S$180

The model is cheap here; the fixed cost dominates. Notice that output tokens cost five times as much as input tokens, so a rule on answer length is also a cost rule.

Operating limits to specify

  • Cost: maximum cost per question and per month, at expected and at high use.
  • Speed: median and p95 response time, and what happens when a step takes too long.
  • Data: where data is processed, what is logged, who can see logs and how long they are kept.
  • Languages and accessibility: which languages are supported, and the reading level.
  • Handover volume: how many conversations people can absorb each day.
  • Change control: who approves changes to sources, instructions or the model, and which tests must pass first.

4.6 Traceability: every requirement has a test

A requirement-to-test table (often called a traceability table) is the backbone of an AI PRD. One row per requirement: its ID, the test set that checks it, the method, the pass threshold and the person who owns it. It does three things: it shows nothing important is untested, it tells the evaluation team what to build in Modules 5 and 6, and it becomes the release gate, the list of tests that must pass before any change goes live.

Keep the document alive. Number your versions, record each change and who approved it, and list open questions honestly. A PRD that says "Open: pharmacy has not yet decided whether to answer in languages without a translated leaflet" is more useful than one that hides the gap.

Step 3 · Assignment · 3–4 hours · Graded 60%The AI requirements document

Your task. Write the requirements document for the medicine questions assistant's limited launch: no more than six pages of text (about 2,500 words), plus Appendix A, a requirement-to-test table covering every requirement. Engineers, pharmacists and testers should all be able to work from it.

What your document must contain

  1. Summary (half a page): the problem, who it is for, the outcome the launch must show, and what version 1 will and will not do.
  2. Users, scope and out of scope: who can use it, which questions are in scope, and what is deliberately left out (for example carer access, medicines not on the list), with reasons.
  3. Behaviour rules: at least 12, each with an ID, a strength (MUST, MUST NEVER, SHOULD PREFER), the situation and the behaviour. Cover all nine pharmacy rules, the problems in the ten transcripts, and identity, language and length.
  4. Refusal, handover and fallback design: which rung of the response ladder each kind of question lands on; the exact patient-facing wording for at least three fixed messages (emergency, handover, fallback); and a handover rule that fits pharmacist capacity at both 18,000 and 60,000 questions a month, or says what must change.
  5. Examples: at least eight example questions with the expected behaviour, including at least three hard or borderline cases.
  6. Operating limits: cost per question and per month at 18,000 and 60,000 questions, against the CFO's cap; median and p95 response time; data, logging and languages. Use the budget calculator below and show your figures.
  7. Release criteria: the tests that must pass before the limited launch, and before any later change goes live. Size the test set for each MUST NEVER using the test-size calculator.
  8. Stakeholder conflicts, risks and open questions: how you resolved each conflict in the pack, what risk remains, and what is still undecided, with an owner.
  9. Appendix A · requirement-to-test table: ID, requirement, test set and size, method, pass threshold and owner, for every requirement. Use the requirement builder below.

How it is marked

  • Covers: everything in the lesson. Expect to rewrite vague requirements as testable ones, choose rule strengths, design a refusal or fallback, calculate a cost per question and a test-set size, on a short new scenario you have not seen.
  • Opens: once both checkpoint quizzes are at mastery. After emailing your assignment, email the course team to request the test paper and book your viva.

Viva · 15%

A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with the living voice") is a short spoken examination. Yours lasts 10 minutes, with an assessor or a trained peer.

MinutesWhat happens
0–2You explain the assistant's scope and its three most important rules in your own words, without reading.
2–7Questions on your choices. Expect "why is this a MUST NEVER and not a MUST?", "how would a tester catch this rule being broken?" and "what happens when the leaflet search fails?"
7–10A new fact you have not seen, such as a price change, a new pharmacy rule or a test result. You say which requirements change, and why.

It is marked on: whether you can defend your choices with evidence, whether you can explain the ideas in plain words, how you handle the new fact, and how clearly you speak. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well.

Practise on your own world

Write five behaviour rules, one fixed refusal message and a requirement-to-test table for one of these product ideas, or for an AI product at your own workplace, and bring it to your viva if you like.

  • Staff information technology (IT) help assistant: what must it never do with passwords, and when does it hand over to the service desk?
  • Trade document checker for a bank: checks shipping and invoice documents against a letter of credit. What must it never decide on its own, and how fast must it be at month end?
