Learn AI Product Management · 14 weeks · No coding required

Most AI projects stall at the decision, not the model

In short
Who it is for
Business analysts, data analysts and product managers who want to lead AI products, plus engineers, designers and domain experts moving into AI product work.
What you learn
How to decide whether to use AI, specify behaviour that varies, test it, pay for it, govern it and defend a launch decision.
What you leave with
Twelve graded work products and a launch decision defended to a mock executive panel.
Time
14 weeks at 6–8 hours a week, about 85–110 hours in total.

Organisations are not short of AI pilots. They are short of people who can turn a pilot into a product that is worth running.

This programme is built for the people best placed to do that: the analysts and product managers who already own requirements, metrics and launch decisions. It adds the AI judgement those roles now demand.

The problem organisations keep paying for

Research by IDC with Lenovo found that only four in every 33 AI proofs of concept reached wide deployment. An MIT study published in August 2025 reported that only around 5% of generative AI pilots were delivering measurable business value at scale.

88%of AI proofs of concept never reach wide deployment (IDC)
~95%of generative AI pilots show no measurable return (MIT NANDA, 2025)
4 of 33pilots reached production in the IDC sample

The reasons these studies give are rarely about the model. They are unclear returns, data that was never ready, weak business cases rushed through under board pressure, and tools that did not fit the real workflow. Each of those is a product decision that someone made, or failed to make, before any code was written.

An AI product succeeds or fails on four questions: should we use AI here, how do we know it is good enough, what does it cost to run, and who is harmed when it is wrong?

Those questions belong to whoever owns the requirements, the metrics and the launch decision. In most organisations that person is a business analyst, a data analyst or a product manager.

What employers are hiring for

AI product roles are not entry-level jobs. An analysis of 12,397 United States AI product postings in 2026 found that 93% were at senior level or above and only 2% were junior. Most asked for around seven years of experience. The capability employers valued most was choosing the right use case for AI.

That is good news if you already have years of analysis or product work behind you. Employers do not want people who know AI and nothing else. They want proven business judgement plus the ability to reason about models, evaluation, cost and risk. LinkedIn's 2026 Jobs on the Rise list points the same way: AI consultants and strategists, with a typical eight years of experience, are among the fastest-growing roles.

The gap this course closes: your experience gets you shortlisted. What decides the interview is whether you can talk precisely about evaluation, unit economics and governance, and show work that proves it.

Three groups in particular each bring something the others lack, and each has a specific gap.

Why this course and not another

You finish with a portfolio, not just a certificate

Every module ends in a work product an employer would recognise: a board memo, a requirements document, an evaluation report, a funding case, a trust pack. By Week 14 you have twelve pieces of evidence that you can do the job.

Evaluation gets two full modules

Most courses treat testing as a footnote. Here you label real outputs, build a failure taxonomy, write an automated judge and set a release threshold. It is the skill hiring managers find hardest to fill.

One realistic case from start to finish

Every assignment uses Harbourview Polyclinics, a fictional Singapore primary care group with five AI initiatives and real tensions between safety, cost and fairness. If your reasoning holds in healthcare, it holds in banking, insurance, retail or logistics. You can use your own product instead if it raises the same questions.

Assessed the way the job is assessed

Each module closes with a viva: ten minutes of questions on your own work, including challenges you did not prepare for. The capstone panel plays a chief financial officer, a chief medical officer and a chief information officer, which is much closer to a real launch review, or a real interview loop, than a multiple-choice exam.

The syllabus draws on Duke University's AI Product Management specialisation, Marily Nika's AI PM bootcamp, Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's evaluation course, Reforge and Product School, and brings their strongest ideas into one sequence with one case.

What it asks of you

Fourteen weeks at six to eight hours a week. Each week follows the same rhythm: a lesson and quiz, a case study, a three-to-four-hour assignment, then a short test and viva. Optional technical labs are there for hands-on practice, and none requires you to write code.

You need no background in machine learning. You do need to be willing to defend your reasoning out loud, because that is what the role demands.

Common questions

I do not code. Will I keep up?

Yes. Every graded deliverable is a written work product. Technical content is taught at the level a product decision needs: what retrieval, fine-tuning and agents are, what they cost and how they fail.

AI changes every few months. Will this go out of date?

Model names will change. The decisions do not: whether to use AI, how to test it, what it costs and how to govern it. Week 11 is specifically about writing a strategy that survives the next model release.

I do not work in healthcare. Is the case relevant?

Patient safety, regulatory scrutiny and fairness across groups have direct equivalents in financial services, insurance, public services and retail. You may substitute your own product for any assignment.

I am already a product manager. What will I gain?

The parts standard product training skips: evaluation design, behaviour specifications, unit economics that move with model prices, and governance documents. These are where experienced product managers most often get caught out in interviews and launch reviews.

How do I show an employer what I learned?

Your module deliverables form a portfolio, and the capstone pack brings them together into one launch recommendation you can walk through in an interview.
